# The first 22 kilometres

*Draft, 12 September 2026. Not published.*

At 8:37 on the morning of 26 August a piece of the north face of Langtang
Lirung let go from about 5,200 metres. It fell around 1,200 metres onto the
loose ground below, scoured that as it went, then dropped another thousand
metres to the stream bed. From there it ran down the Lhende Khola. A camera at
the Rasuwagadhi border post, 22 kilometres away, filmed it arriving 7 minutes
and 40 seconds later.

That works out at about 170 km/h on average. Seismic tracking of the mass from 16 nearby stations, published in September, breaks that into legs: very fast off the headwall, faster still to the big bend at kilometre 6.6, then about 39 metres a second for the last 14 kilometres. So it accelerated and then slowed. I had said it did not slow down, which was wrong.

## Dry rock cannot do that

Take the drop and divide it by the distance run. About 3,100 metres down over
22 kilometres gives 0.14. For anything sliding to a stop that ratio is its
friction, so a mass only gets 22 km at all if its friction is under 0.14.

Dry rock at this size has a friction of about 0.28 to 0.30. That is a standard
regression, not a guess. Rubble released from 5,200 metres would have stopped
after about 10 kilometres, in the upper valley, well short of the border. This
went twice as far and was still moving fast when it got there.

So it was not dry rock. Something made it roughly twice as slippery as rock
has any business being.

## Ice, and the physics of a skate

Ice. Not melted into water first, but as ice.

Three things happen at once. An ice surface carries a thin disordered film
even with nothing pressing on it. Sliding heats the contact points, and at
these speeds enough heat is made to keep a film melted. And the ice breaks up
as it travels, so the mass keeps making fresh slippery surfaces instead of
polishing one.

This is not a new idea and it is not mine. The closest thing on record is
Kolka-Karmadon in the Caucasus in 2002: 100 to 130 million cubic metres,
mostly glacier ice, which ran 18 to 20 kilometres at 50 to 80 metres a second
and then stopped dead where the valley geometry stopped it. Langtang covered a
similar distance at a similar speed.

Worth separating two things here, because I ran them together at first. That
ice did the lubricating is well supported — nothing else gets a mass this far.
How much ice there was is a different question, and a much weaker one.
Chamoli in India in 2021 was only about a fifth ice and still ran 25
kilometres. The lubricant does not have to be the bulk of the thing.

## There was almost no river up there

The Lhende is small. About 150 cubic metres a second where it meets the big
river, and less higher up. All the water standing in its 22 kilometres of
channel that morning came to roughly half a million cubic metres. Add the
water squeezed out of the gravel it tore up, and what friction had melted by
then, and 2 to 4 million is the ceiling.

The mass that came down was tens of millions. So what hit the border post was
at least four fifths mountain. "A wall of water" is the wrong description of
it, and I used that phrase myself for the first week.

The water story starts *at* the border junction, not above it. Below there the
river holds about 22 million cubic metres over its next 177 kilometres, and
that is what the flood was mostly made of. Marc Hendrickx, working
independently on the same 22 kilometres, gets the same answer about the river
up there being negligible, and his model stops at the border for the same
reason mine changes character there.

## How big, and what of

Running the model across the range other people have published, and keeping
only the versions that match everything measured above Betrawati at once — the
clock, the speed in the gorge, how deep the water got at four separate places,
how much valley floor was scoured out, and how much rock was left behind — the
answer comes out at **140 to 160 million cubic metres**, released over about
four and a half to seven and a half minutes.

That is a slab roughly 140 to 160 metres thick over the area that broke away.
Chamoli in India in 2021 was about 135 metres thick by the same arithmetic. So
it is a large number but not a strange one.

On what it was made of, I cannot tell you, and it is worth being clear about
why.

The obvious guess, and the one I made first, is that it was mostly ice. That
is what makes a mass this mobile, and it is what the model prefers. But the
preference comes from one measurement and nothing else.

Satellites measured about 12 million cubic metres of rock left behind in the
valley. If only that much rock stopped, not much rock can have come down, and
the model is pushed hard towards ice. Take that one constraint away and leave
everything else — the clock, the speed in the gorge, all four depth
measurements, the scour, the arrival at Syabrubesi — and the model stops
caring. The middle of the range drops from about 90 per cent ice to about 45
per cent, and of the versions that pass everything else, more than half have
less ice than rock.

So the honest statement is that the first 22 kilometres do not tell you what
fell. Everything I have that points at ice is a single rock-deposit figure
which is a lower bound, taken from less than half the valley floor, and which
will move when someone surveys the rest of it. Marc Hendrickx, working the
same 22 kilometres independently, uses 13 per cent ice and gets a working
model. So does mine, if I relax that one number.

What does hold either way is the size. The volume comes out at 140 to 160
million cubic metres whatever I assume about the ice, which is the one thing
here I would defend.

## What would settle it

Two surveys of the deposit a month apart. If it is mostly ice it must keep
losing volume as the buried ice melts out. Rock does not do that. Nobody needs
a model to read that measurement, and the window for taking it is open now.

## What I am not claiming

This is the first 22 kilometres only. Below the border the same model does not
work yet — it puts about 15 metres of water at Galchhi where the gauge says
around 9, and I have not fixed that. Seven attempts have failed, and on
8 September I stopped and split the problem in two rather than try an eighth.
This is the half that fits.

Six passing runs out of 300 is thin. One input, the width of the upper valley,
is not constrained at all by these tests. Every number here is preliminary,
none of it is peer reviewed, and no Nepali scientist has read any of it.
