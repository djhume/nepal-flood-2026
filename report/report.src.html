The Trishuli Water Ledger

On 26 August 2026 a collapsing mountainside above the Lhende Khola, in Rasuwa, sent a wall of ice, rock and mud — 70 metres high in the gorges — into the Bhote Koshi, and a flood down 199 km of the Bhote Koshi and the Trishuli, killing more than 1,340 people and leaving nearly 5,000 missing. Early accounts lead with frictionally melted glacier ice. The arithmetic says most of the water was already in the river.

Entrainment was built, and the erosion volume lands. The model could deposit sediment but never entrain any. Two closures were added (Takahashi capacity, Frank et al. shear) with every coefficient taken from the literature and nothing fitted to this event. Result: 3.8 Mm³ of erosion against geopera's 3.2 Mm³ measured by WorldView-3 stereo, in the reaches they mapped, robust across closure, scenario, erodible-layer depth and settling cap.

The single-phase model was then falsified. Rejection sampling over five contested inputs (release volume log-uniform 1–200 Mm³, liquid fraction, μdry, Manning scale, erodible depth) found 0 of 150 samples able to satisfy the upper-corridor clocks and the corridor mass balance simultaneously. Border arrival and deposition were satisfied together by zero runs; every other pair by 16–41. That falsifies the model form, not the parameters.

The fix is two-phase solids. Coarse load obeys the capacity closure, strands, and carries the granular friction; fine load and ice are advected with the water, are rheologically part of the fluid (the dial sees weff = (hw+hf)/h), and do not deposit in this domain. With ffine = 0 the published tables reproduce bit-identically. geopera — Geopera Pty Ltd, the analysis by its founder Darcy Weedman, a company blog and not peer-reviewed — reached the same conclusion the same week by gradient-based calibration of a 2D morphodynamic model, which drove their settling velocity down by a factor of three. Two unreviewed analyses agreeing is corroboration, not replication, and we cite it on those terms throughout.

An envelope on event size. 220 Latin-hypercube samples over six inputs, scored against five observables: 26 fit, giving a release of 14–34 Mm³ (median 21) against Kargel 50–200, Azam/ICIMOD 100–200, geopera 60–140 and EGU 0.5–10. Relaxing the deposition cap by 2.4× moved the envelope ~30% and the ranges overlap, so it is not an artefact of that constraint. Only the volume is constrained — liquid fraction, μdry and ffine each span >90% of their priors, and earlier tighter values for them are retracted. One input in that scoring is exposed: the border speed observable is 48.5 ± 35% m/s, taken from the superelevation estimate, and the first peer-reviewed study of the event — a Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) frame-by-frame video analysis published 1 September — measures 19 m/s at the same location, which lies outside that tolerance. That re-score was run late on 6 September: against 19 m/s, 0 of 200 samples pass, because no release at any size can make the border arrival and a 19 m/s border speed together — consistent with 19 being the post-turn water surface rather than the front, and equally consistent with our speed-to-arrival physics being wrong; see §08 for why three instruments plausibly measure three different things at a junction. And late on 6 September two measurements that share none of these observables — the volume that ran up the Chinese arm, and a 45–70 m mud line at the Upper Trishuli-1 headworks where the passing runs put 5–21 m — both said this envelope is probably too small. They are preliminary and set out in §12b; the envelope stands with that caveat attached.

An out-of-sample test, half passed. Galchhi and Devghat were held out of the scoring. The 26 passing runs give a Devghat peak of 1,752–2,889 against ~2,900 observed — 100% within a factor of 2, which was the stated pass criterion, but note that every passing run lands below the observation, so this is "not contradicted" rather than "reproduced" and the residual is one-sided. The Galchhi 30-minute rise is 1.8–6.1 m against ~9 (23% of the passing runs within a factor of 2). The model routes the right discharge and converts it to the wrong stage, which implicates lower-reach channel geometry — below km 60 our widths come from hydraulic geometry, not measurement.

Added 6 September. A description, not an argument — assembled from the evidence dossier so that a reader meets the event before the arithmetic about it. Every number here is sourced in research/event-dossier.md; where sources disagree the range is given.

The mountain, and what let go

Langtang Lirung is a 7,227 m peak about 60 km north of Kathmandu, in Rasuwa district. Its north face stands above the Chhochen Khola, a headwater of the Lhende Khola, about 4 km inside Nepal. At 08:37:10 on the morning of 26 August 2026 — clear, rain-free, in the last weeks of the monsoon — a section of that face roughly 600 m wide and 0.2 km² in area failed at about 5,200 m (reports place it between 5,000 and 5,800; the 4,928 m at km 0 of the profile above is the DEM elevation at the scar coordinate where the modelled path starts, not the crown of the failure). The signal was large enough to be logged as an earthquake: USGS first reported magnitude 4.4 and reclassified it as a 5.2 generated by the collapse itself; GFZ recorded Mw 5.7. One waveform reading (geopera) sees five pulses inside the spike — a staged, domino-style failure.

What fell was bedrock carrying glacier ice. Every analyst who has looked at the imagery describes it that way — Shugar: "a big bedrock failure that took part of the glacier with it"; GFZ: "break-off of a hanging glacier and underlying bedrock"; Steiner: the lower part of a glacier tongue "sheared off because the rock below failed"; ICIMOD's Azam: bedrock failure with the glacier swept along. USGS declined to choose between a landslide and a glacial collapse. A drone at a Chinese radar station filmed it live: a white stream leading a brown dust cloud. Nobody has published the ice fraction. The volume is disputed by a factor of four hundred — 0.5–10 Mm³ for the initial detachment (EGU preliminary), 50–200 (Kargel), 100–200 (Azam/ICIMOD); this report's own envelope is 14–34 Mm³ (§00). Whether the face gave warning is contested: one InSAR analysis reads accelerating creep in the days before; another reads under two metres of steady motion in a hundred days with no acceleration at all.

The descent

The mass fell about 1,200 m (Steiner reads ~2,100 to the damming point) onto the ground below the face — proglacial moraine and loose debris, according to the HiRISK rapid assessment and the Chinese agency reconstructions, which have the debris avalanche scouring moraine from ~5,200 down to ~4,000 m; the often-repeated line that it "fell onto the flatter glacier below" is unattributed, and no post-event measurement of that ground has been published — then a further ~1,000 m to the channel of the Lhende, some 2,200–2,500 m below the scar in total. Lower down it ran over the still-wet deposits of the July 2025 outburst flood. The one peer-reviewed study so far (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Institute of Mountain Hazards and Environment, frame-by-frame video) classifies the cascade as ice avalanche → debris flow → mudslide → flood and puts the transition to debris flow at about 4,000 m. The avalanche left a barrier dam in the upper Lhende — the CAS study gives ~60 m high and ~1,100 m long, impounding ~2.2 Mm³ — which overtopped on 28 August and had largely drained by the 30th, plus a smaller pond about 2.4 km from the source. ICIMOD proposed that the avalanche briefly dammed the monsoon-swollen Lhende during the event and that the impoundment burst; Sinclair objected that the border arrival is too fast for that to be the primary mechanism, and our own arithmetic agrees that a minutes-scale impoundment can re-time a pulse but cannot supply one.

Twenty-two kilometres to the border

From the Chhochen the flow ran down the Lhende, which from about km 8 is the international boundary itself, Nepal on one bank and Tibet on the other. (The distance is 22.0 km along the channel we traced; the CAS paper gives 21.7, China's Ministry of Natural Resources "about 20", one CAS news item "about 15", Xinhua and Kargel "about 22".) The mean speed of the front over the 22 km was 47.8 m/s (§03). geopera's superelevation work gives 37 m/s in the upper Lhende and 45–52 at the border; their trimlines in the gorge run 40–134 m above the river, median about 70 — mud lines that mix flow depth with the run-up of a fast flow driven into a wall, and have to be read with that in mind (§08). Satellite stereo shows the gorge floor was lowered 2–12 m along this reach: the flow eroded here, it did not deposit.

At 08:44:50 it reached the T-junction where the Lhende meets the main Kyirong Tsangpo–Trishuli stem at 96°. Gyirong Port, the Chinese crossing, sat on the far bank directly opposite the mouth; Rasuwagadhi customs and the 111 MW Rasuwagadhi hydropower plant sat on the Nepali side, the plant's headworks 400 m below the junction (49 of its staff are missing). The port's own security camera recorded the arrival, and Chinese reporting described what it shows as "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks". Read directly off that footage, the front crossed two surveyed sight lines 50 m apart in 1.066 s — about 47 m/s, with an envelope of 41–63 (§03). A six-storey building on the port terrace was not damaged but erased, which puts a floor of 18–20 m under the flow depth; a vegetation trimline on the sheltered side sits 60 m above the 1,815 m river bed; and the cliff the flow struck square-on carries a mark at 1,920–1,930 m — 45–55 m of run-up above the sheltered surface, which is the velocity head of material moving at about 30 m/s. Depth, then, about 60 m; the material at ~30 m/s; the front at ~47–54. Part of the flow drove up the Chinese arm of the valley: China's Ministry of Natural Resources said part of the flow ran "nearly 3 km upstream in the Gyirong Tsangpo", Xinhua reported about 3 km of the G216 highway approaching the port damaged, a contour trace of the stagnation elevation runs ~3.5 km up that arm, and Sentinel-2 change measured along the river centreline (6 September) has the stripped ground fading out between 2 and 3.5 km from the junction while the two through-flow arms stay stripped for the whole 5–8 km in view — four lines converging on about 3 km for the surge, with fresh sediment visible in sub-metre imagery to about 4.6 km that may be ponding rather than run-up (§08).

Down the Bhote Koshi

Below the junction the river is Nepal's Bhote Koshi. Timure, the dry port at km 26, and the Friendship Bridge — both rebuilt after a glacial-lake outburst down the same corridor on 8 July 2025 — went again. At Syabrubesi (km 37.6) the DHM gauge read 3.8 m and rising at 08:50 and then stopped transmitting; the town itself is reconstructed as inundated at 09:10–09:25. Here the valley opens, the superelevation speed collapses from ~50 m/s to about 11, and the Langtang Khola comes in from the east (the Langtang Khola hydropower plant: 42 missing). Just below, at km 40–43, is the main deposit the flood left: a sheet 3 km long and 12–18 m thick, carrying 4,329 boulders over a metre across in 37 hectares, the largest 26 m; the river has since cut a 13–21 m trench through it. Over the 45% of the valley floor the stereo survey could see, erosion (3.2 Mm³) outweighed deposition (0.9) by more than three to one — both lower bounds. The Chilime plant's tunnel and powerhouse were damaged. At Betrawati (km 68) the gauge read 3.55 m at 09:20 and was lost; the peak passed about 10:30. Below Betrawati the river is the Trishuli, and the corridor was full of hydropower workers: some 300 were inside the Upper Trishuli-1 tunnels, of whom about 350 across UT-1 and 208 from Trishuli-III were brought out alive over the following days; Upper Trishuli 3A and 3B and the 220 kV substation were destroyed. Four DHM telemetry stations were destroyed in all.

The Trishuli, Devghat, and India

Galchhi (km 108), the gauge that survived, rose about 9 m in 30 minutes around 11:00. Malekhu (km 117) crossed its warning level at 11:20 and its danger level at 11:40, reached 10.48 m, and lost its bridge at 11:43. Kalikhola near Muglin (km 185) crossed danger at 14:14. The flood reached Devghat (km 199), where the Trishuli meets the Kali Gandaki, at 15:20 and peaked at 16:00 — 6.57 m, 5,850 m³/s — and was near normal by 18:30; the ~20 Mm³ of "excess" water the Flood Forecasting Division quotes is the flow above base at that station between 14:10 and 18:00. Across the Indian border the Valmikinagar barrage on the Gandak recorded a flat peak of 4,253 m³/s at 23:00–23:30 IST. Bodies and debris were recovered 200–240 km downstream. A second landslide-classified seismic event (M 4.2) was recorded at 11:45, three hours after the first; whether it was a second failure or the barrier dam is unresolved. SMS alerts went to 679,295 riverbank residents at 09:15 — 38 minutes after the collapse, and after the wave had already passed everything above Syabrubesi.

The toll, and the corridor's history

As of 5 September: 1,342 dead and 4,886 missing in Nepal, 31 dead and 531 missing on the Chinese side, more than 5,300 injured. Thirteen hydropower projects totalling 748 MW were damaged and 431 MW came off the grid. This is the same mountain whose south flank fell in the 2015 earthquake and buried Langtang village, 7 km from the 2026 scar in a different valley, leaving a measured 6.8 Mm³ deposit; and the same corridor that carried the July 2025 outburst from a supraglacial lake on the Purepu glacier.

What is not known The volume and ice fraction of what fell. Whether the glacier below the scar was excavated (no post-event measurement of it exists). The hydrographs at the four destroyed gauges. How far the flow went up the Chinese arm. What the flow consisted of, station by station, between the junction and Syabrubesi. Where, along the corridor, the dead were. Whether the 11:45 seismic event was a second collapse. Every one of these is an input this report would rather measure than assume, and §13 says what would settle each.

01The question

When part of Langtang Lirung's north face let go — bedrock plus a hanging glacier; published guesses now span ~1–10 million m³ for the initial detachment to an unsourced 100–200 million m³ total, and nobody has yet published a firm volume or ice fraction — the mass fell about 1,200 m, hit the valley floor, and arrived at the China–Nepal border 22 km away seven minutes and forty seconds later (six minutes fifty until 6 September — see §00). What followed down the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli was not a landslide but a flood: a sediment-charged wave that gauges tracked all the way to the Narayani confluence.

The question this page tries to answer with arithmetic rather than adjectives: where did that much water come from? The narrative that leads most coverage — inherited from the well-studied 2021 Chamoli disaster — is that the fall's energy frictionally melted the glacier ice. Our hypothesis, formed watching the videos: the debris wave acted as a moving dam, sweeping up the monsoon river that was already in the channel, kilometre after kilometre, and that cumulative river water dominates the ledger.

To be fair to the scientists quoted in the press: Jeff Kargel's own account names "melted avalanche ice, entrained sediment and the river itself", and Dave Petley's is similar. Nobody serious is ignoring river water. But nobody has published the relative fractions either — and the fractions are the whole story. Dan Shugar put it plainly: "Figuring out where that water came from is actually probably the hardest thing about all this."

The human cost 1,342 people are confirmed dead in Nepal and 4,886 missing, with 31 dead and 531 missing on the Chinese side, and 5,300+ injured (5 September count). The missing figure has begun to fall while the death toll rises: that is recovery, not rescue. Of ~900 bodies recovered by 1 September, about 4% had been identified. Read every number here as provisional. Among them are hundreds of hydropower workers — though some 560 were brought out of the Upper Trishuli-1 and Trishuli-III tunnels alive in the days that followed. The wave carried away bridges, vehicles, homes and people; no term in this arithmetic stands for them. We do this arithmetic because where the water comes from determines what a warning system must watch for, and the 38 minutes between collapse and SMS alert were not enough. 02The corridor

Geography first, because it is routinely reported wrongly. The collapse was in Nepal — the scar sits about 4 km inside the international boundary, on the north face of Langtang Lirung, and fell into the Chhochen Khola, a headwater of the Lhende. But from roughly km 8 the Lhende is the boundary: over the next 14 km the modelled path never departs from the mapped border by more than 180 m, Nepal on one bank and Tibet on the other. The Lhende then dead-ends into the main Kyirong Tsangpo–Trishuli stem — a river that has itself come out of Tibet — at the crossing where Gyirong Port sat directly opposite the mouth, on the far bank from Rasuwagadhi. So the flood was transboundary within eight minutes, and a substantial share of the water it swept up had entered the system on the Chinese side of the boundary — which matters for the ledger below, because that water is part of the standing channel volume the wave collected. From there the river is Nepal's Bhote Koshi, then the Trishuli, gathering tributaries until it meets the Kali Gandaki at Devghat to form the Narayani. The junctions on this map are more than scenery: when the surge arrives, an entire side arm's storage suddenly connects and the flow pays a momentum toll to turn — a piecewise step in the system, of which the border T-junction is the first and sharpest (§08).

Reading the camera: the clock, the calibration, and a direct speed

Added 6 September. Method and measurement, logged in the changelog rather than promoted to a finding. Full working: research/event-dossier.md §14; tooling in research/video/. The footage is third-party and not in the repository.

The footage. A 1280×720 upload of the Gyirong Port CCTV, 66.9 s long, carrying the station's own burnt-in clock. Its frame rate was the first thing we got wrong: a perceptual duplicate-frame filter reported 23.85 unique frames per second, and a strict pixel-identity test gave 48–57 — 57 of 60 during the arrival. One frame is 16.7 ms, not the 33 ms an earlier 30 fps resample had assumed, and that resample was itself throwing away half the information. Everything below uses the original frames at their original timestamps.

The clock. The overlay ticks in whole seconds, but the tick edges are detectable and fall at video t = n + 0.100 s; the gaps between detected digit changes were 3.033, 0.967 and 2.000 s, all within 35 ms of exact integers, which is what shows the clip is real-time and uncut. That pins video t = 0 to 10:59:25.900 Beijing, ±35 ms. First dust at the left of frame at 10:59:47.5; the black plume emerges from behind the building at video 24.700 s — 10:59:50.6 Beijing, 08:44:50.6 Nepal — which is the arrival used in the table above. One caveat the footage cannot close: the CCTV clock's own drift is unknown.

The calibration, two ways. The border-post facade is mirror-symmetric about the national emblem, and the dentil frieze under its roofline is a graduated scale: Dave Hume counted 18 squares from the centre square to the building's end, over a 33.5 m half-length, giving a 1.97 m module (a 2 m architectural set-out; automated peak detection found the same 18, with the centre square within one pixel of the emblem). A projective fit to those graduations had an rms of 1.97 px. Independently, he surveyed the two building ends from the camera position in Google Earth: bearing 167.82° at 117 m and 194.00° at 145 m.

04The energy wedge

Melting ice is expensive: 334 kJ per kilogram. A kilogram of anything falling 1,200 m releases just 11.8 kJ. So even if every joule of the initial fall became heat and every joule of that heat went into ice, the collapse could melt no more than ~3.5% of its own mass. Frictional melt is energy-limited, not ice-limited — it doesn't matter how much ice fell; it matters how far it fell.

We tested this bookkeeping against the one well-instrumented analogue. For Chamoli 2021 (26.9 Mm³, 80:20 rock:ice, 3,400 m fall — winter, rivers near empty), our budget reproduces the Science paper's published answer: ~5 Mm³ of meltwater, ice-limited, with river water a ~8% term. Shugar, Kargel and colleagues called that rock:ice ratio "almost exactly the critical value required for near-complete melting" — a rare coincidence, not a general rule.

04bIce and rock together are not rock: the first 22 kilometres

Added 6 September, evening. An interpretation and some arithmetic, not a scored result; it changes how findings 01 and 02 are worded, not what they say. Prompted by Dave Hume's question of whether the first 22 km carried much water at all.

How much water was there to sweep up above the border?

Very little. The Lhende is a small river. The baseflow the routing model carries (model/snowplow.py) runs from 2 m³/s at the scar to 30 at km 5 and 150 at the border; at a monsoon velocity of 3 m/s that is a wetted section of 1–50 m², and the water standing in the whole 22 km of channel comes to about 0.5 Mm³. The river's inflow during the 7.7 minutes the front took to pass adds 0.07. The saturated bed the flow tore up in the gorge — the entrainment ledger puts it near 2.6 Mm³ of bulk material above the border — releases perhaps 0.9 Mm³ of pore water. Frictional melt is capped at 1.1–2.5 Mm³ for the whole event (§04), and most of that is produced late, because heat takes minutes to soak into ice before it melts it (§10). Add every term and the water available in the Lhende reach is 2–4 Mm³, against a release of 14–34 Mm³ of rock and ice. What struck Gyirong Port was, by volume, at least four-fifths the mountain itself. "A black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks" is the description of a debris flow, and that is what the mud lines at the junction — 60 m deep, material at ~30 m/s, a front at ~47–54 (§08) — are describing: a rock–ice mass hitting a wall, not a flood.

The water story begins at the junction. The main stem arriving from Tibet carries ~150 m³/s, the corridor below holds ~22 Mm³ standing in the channel at any instant (§04), and the snowplow of §05 is a description of what happened from km 22 onward. Finding 01 is unchanged in the ledger and clearer in geography: above the junction there was almost no river to sweep; below it there were 22 million cubic metres.

So what got 14–34 million cubic metres down 22 km in 7 min 40 s?

Ice. Not as water, as ice. The simplest way to see it uses no model at all. For a mass sliding from the scar to the junction floor — about 3,100 m of descent over 22 km — the ratio of drop to run is H/L = 0.14. A sliding block arrives with speed v² = 2g(H − μL), so it arrives at all only if its effective friction coefficient is below 0.14, and it arrives at 47 m/s, still moving, only if μ is very close to that — about 0.135. Scheidegger's 1973 volume–mobility law, the standard rule for dry rock avalanches, gives μ ≈ 0.30 at 21 Mm³ and 0.28 at 34: a dry rock avalanche of this size stops after about 10 km. That is finding 02 restated without a routing model, and it is why the dry-rock scenarios in the model arrive at 17–30 minutes only by borrowing water from the channel on the way down.

Rock–ice avalanches are a different class of event, and the literature is more careful than a slogan. In rotating-drum experiments Schneider, Kaitna and colleagues (2011) found granular ice cuts the bulk friction of a gravel mass by up to 20% and water by about 50%, with mixtures above ~40% ice "nearly completely" liquefying as the ice fragments and melts; in their field set of 64 rock–ice avalanches over a million cubic metres, the same group (Schneider, Huggel, Haeberli and Kaitna 2011) could not confirm the ice fraction as a control on runout — what showed up was water, volume, and travel over a glacier. Back-analyses of rock avalanches running on glaciers give Voellmy friction coefficients of 0.03–0.10 (Sosio et al. 2012), and ice-rich glacier detachments reach angles down to about 5°, a drop-to-run ratio of 0.09 (Kääb et al. 2021) — far below the 0.28–0.30 Scheidegger gives dry rock at this size. The model's ice-rich scenarios use μ = 0.17, which the code attributes to Schneider et al.'s calibrations; we could not open that paper's full text on 6 September to confirm the figure, and say so. The closest analogue is Kolka–Karmadon, Caucasus, 20 September 2002: 100–130 million cubic metres, roughly 80–90% glacier ice with about 10% water in the mass, that ran 18–20 km down the Genaldon valley at a mean 50–80 m/s — four to six minutes, by that arithmetic; the papers do not quote the elapsed time — with a drop-to-run ratio of 0.15 from the wall to the Karmadon gates, where the debris stalled and the liquid fraction ran on "immediately" for another 15 km as a mudflow (Huggel et al. 2005; Haeberli et al. 2004; Evans et al. 2009). The Lhende run is that class of event at a fifth of the size: a similar distance at a similar mean speed, a similar drop-to-run ratio, a debris body that stopped where the valley geometry stopped it, and a flood that continued. Huascarán 1970 is the other classic — 50–80 million cubic metres, 13–16 km to Yungay in three to four and a half minutes, its mobility attributed to the glacial till, snow and meltwater it entrained (Evans et al. 2009; Mergili et al. 2018). Chamoli 2021, at 80% rock, is the contrast: a similar drop-to-run ratio but a front that had slowed to ~25 m/s by 15 km and 12–16 by 25 km, 34–37 minutes to Tapovan, which is what a mostly-rock mass with a thin ice budget does (§10). One more piece of arithmetic from that paper carries over: a 3,100 m drop can melt at most about a fifth of a mass's volume if that fifth is ice, so anything ice-richer than Chamoli arrives at the bottom still carrying ice — which is what the border footage shows and what the 50,000 m³ of ice debris reported in the barrier lake suggests.

Why ice does this — the physics of an ice skate

Three mechanisms, in the order that matters at avalanche speeds.

Premelting. An ice surface carries a disordered, liquid-like film with no load on it at all (Faraday noticed it in the 1850s; Dash, Rempel and Wettlaufer's 2006 review is the modern account). The film thickens toward 0 °C and is why a skate glides at −30 °C, where pressure melting is impossible — the pressure effect is 0.0074 °C per atmosphere with a hard floor of about 22 °C, as the correction of 6 September on the plain-English page sets out. Premelting is the reason ice is slippery at rest.

Frictional melting at the base. This is the one that runs an avalanche. The power dissipated per square metre of sliding contact is the shear stress times the slip rate, μ σ v. Under 30 m of debris (σ ≈ 0.6 MPa), with μ = 0.1 and v = 40 m/s, that is about 2.4 MW per square metre — enough to melt 7 mm of ice per second at the contact, whatever the bulk temperature of the ice. The water film renews itself faster than it can be squeezed out, and it does so from the moment the mass is moving fast. Over the whole run this consumes a few per cent of the fall's energy: it is lubrication, not water production, and the bulk-melt ceiling of §04 is untouched by it. It is also velocity-weakening — faster sliding makes more melt, which lowers μ, which makes it faster — the same runaway that produces frictional melt on fault surfaces, and a standard result in the ice-friction literature (Kietzig, Hatzikiriakos and Englezos 2010 review it). A law like that predicts a front that accelerates to a plateau and holds it, which is what the Lhende data show: ~47 m/s at km 22 against a 47.8 m/s mean over the 22 km, no deceleration down the gorge. A constant-friction mass would have slowed steadily. That observation is already sitting in the record, and PLAN.md §8a logs the rate-dependent friction law as the next refinement of the model, deferred because it would not move the size envelope.

Fluidisation. Ice fragments melting inside the moving mass raise pore pressure and turn a grinding granular body into a slurry that carries its own weight on its water — the two-phase mechanics Pudasaini and Krautblatter (2014) built for exactly this class of event. This is the process the model's friction dial parameterises: μ falls with the fluid fraction w, and since the two-phase fix of 4 September ice counts on the fluid side of that dial (model/ENTRAINMENT.md). So the ensemble's "liquid fraction" should be read as ice-plus-water fraction, and the runs that pass are runs with enough ice or water to lubricate — not necessarily wet ones. The site said "wet or icy"; that was right. The plain-English page said "only something running like slush" could make the border in time; that was too strong and has been corrected.

What kind of event it was, and what the plateau measures

Added 7 September. In the classification everyone uses (Varnes, revised by Hungr and colleagues in 2014) a slide moves as a coherent body on a shear surface and decelerates once it has spent its head, because its friction does not depend on speed; an avalanche or flow is fragmented, behaves as a fluid, and accelerates until drag balances gravity, then holds a terminal speed. Confinement is the formal line between two of the flow classes: a debris avalanche runs unconfined on a slope, a debris flow surges down a channel. So the first 22 km were a rock–ice avalanche where the mass left the face, a debris avalanche on the proglacial slope below it, and a channelised debris flow once the Lhende confined it — which is the sequence the Chinese post-event reports give — at a speed that belongs to the rock–ice avalanche class: ordinary debris flows run at 5–20 m/s, this one at ~45. The speed record (about 45 m/s in the Lhende gorge by the trimline map's bend pairs, 45–52 at the border, 47 off the video, 47.8 as the 22-km mean) is a flow that reached its running speed within the first few kilometres and held it; a slide would have slowed steadily over the remaining twenty, as Chamoli's mostly-rock mass did.

What this changes, and what it does not

It changes words. "Wall of water" was the wrong description of what reached km 22, and the pages now say ice, rock and mud where they mean the first 22 km and a flood where they mean what followed. It reframes finding 02: "not dry rock" means the run down the Lhende is what a rock–ice avalanche does on its own; it did not need the river, and the Lhende had almost no river to offer. It sharpens finding 01: the river water was collected below the junction, and that is where the ledger's 22 Mm³ sits. It does not change the size envelope, which is pinned by the clock, the erosion volume and the deposition cap, none of which cares how μ got low, only that it did. It does not settle the ice fraction, which remains unpublished — and that is the one number that could overturn this reading: a measured fraction near or below Chamoli's 20% would mean a mostly-rock mass covered 22 km at 47.8 m/s, which the Chamoli record says does not happen without a water supply nobody has yet identified. The Lhende's own contribution is small on every reading: the only measured flow in its upper reach is the 23 m³/s inflow to the barrier lake on 30 August. And it adds nothing to the warning problem except emphasis: an ice-rich face above a monsoon river produces a front at 170 km/h regardless of what the river is doing.

Three checks that would test this reading The front's speed profile down the Lhende. Velocity-weakening friction predicts a plateau; constant friction predicts steady deceleration. One point (km 22) is consistent with the plateau; geopera's 37 m/s in the upper Lhende and 45–52 at the border are consistent with acceleration to it. A speed at km 10–15 would discriminate. The deposit at km 40–43. If it is ice-rich it must go on losing volume for weeks as buried ice melts out; rock does not. Two stereo surveys a month apart would separate them (already in §13). The ground below the scar. Every account has the mass leaving the glacier tongue and scouring proglacial moraine to ~4,000 m; a clear-sky DEM difference would show how much it took, and whether any ice went with it. No such measurement exists.

05The snowplow model

To go beyond static budgets we built a one-dimensional routing model along the real channel: 499 elevation samples down the stitched OSM river path (Copernicus- derived terrain via the Mapzen dataset). The front advances at a speed set by local slope plus a flood-wave floor, U = 300·S0.82 + 4 m/s, calibrated against the clocks above. As it advances it integrates the river: standing channel water is swept into the wave, baseflow keeps arriving, pore water is liberated from the bed the flow tears up, and the meltwater slug from the steep upper reach rides along. The wave body travels slower than the front (fitted factor 0.90), so the pulse stretches — about 45 minutes long at the border, ~3.3 hours long at Devghat, matching the FFD's observed 15:20 arrival → 16:00 peak → 18:30 return to normal — and the peak decays except where re-fed.

07Gross versus net — the subtlety in the 20 Mm³

One bookkeeping distinction does a lot of work. Water the wave merely redistributes — channel water concentrated from 199 km of river into a three-hour pulse — shows up at a gauge as a huge surge, but over a day-scale window it nets toward zero: the emptied channel refills from the baseflow behind it. Genuinely new water — melt, and pore water squeezed from entrained sediment — does not cancel. Our snowplow scenario puts the gross surge at ~65 Mm³ and net-new water at ~10 Mm³.

The FFD's press release resolves what their "20 million cubic metres of excess" means: it is the flow above base level measured at Devghat between 14:10 and 18:00 — a windowed gross excess at one station, marked preliminary. That definition captures redistributed channel water that arrived inside the window and misses the long tail. Run their arithmetic backwards and it also reveals the gauge: 20 Mm³ over the ~3.8-hour window is a mean excess of ~1,450 m³/s — a triangular peak excess of ~2,900 — implying a base flow near 2,950 m³/s at the gauge. That is the Narayani below the Kali Gandaki confluence (Trishuli ~1,500 plus Kali Gandaki ~1,450), not the Trishuli arm alone — a reading their numbers make mutually consistent, and one that changes what "peak 5,850" demands of a routing model (§08, §13). Either way, the gap between the gross wave and the net-new water is itself the evidence: redistribution, not creation, is what made this flood enormous. A melt-only event has no such gap.

08The river as a circuit

There is a second way to see all of this, natural to anyone who works on power systems. Drop the inertia term from the shallow-water equations and what remains — the diffusive wave approximation used in flood routing since the 1930s — is mathematically identical to a nonlinear RC transmission line: the telegrapher's equations without the inductor. Stage is voltage, discharge is current, Manning friction is a nonlinear series resistor, valley storage is shunt capacitance (tiny in the gorges, huge where valleys open — the observed velocity collapse from ~50 to ~11 m/s at Syabrubesi is a fat shunt capacitor clipping the pulse). Side valleys are RC branches: the passing surge drives water up each tributary mouth (charging), which drains back after the peak — clipping the crest and feeding the tail. A temporary debris dam is a breakdown element — a capacitor behind a rising resistance that fails at threshold, like an SCR firing; a chain of them is a relaxation-oscillator cascade, which is what the 2012 Seti flood's ~27 successive surges look like.

What the upgrade did not fix is equally informative: the Devghat peak barely moved, and sensitivity runs (double resolution, narrower channel) show the remaining distal gap is not the L and not the numerics — it is the C of the lower line (how much storage per kilometre the last 80 km really has) and, we now suspect, partly the observation itself: the FFD's own volume-and-duration arithmetic implies the Devghat gauge sits on the combined Narayani with a base flow near 2,900 m³/s (§07), which would put the true observed excess peak near 2,900 rather than 4,350 m³/s — between the two models. Pinning that gauge down is now a top data task (§13).

What the border cameras probably saw. If the 08:44 CCTV frames captured the junction impact — the debris field slamming the far wall and surging up the main valley — rather than a down-valley front, then the fastest "border" speed estimates describe the end of the Lhende descent, not the start of the Trishuli run, and a brief up-valley flow (a reversal, from a camera's point of view) is entirely plausible. That single geometric fact would reconcile our stubbornest data conflict: 45–52 m/s measured at the junction, ~21 m/s averaged beyond it — the junction ate the difference, as a step response. And the first peer-reviewed study of the event — a Chinese Academy of Sciences frame-by-frame video analysis published 1 September — now measures the surface velocity at the Gyirong Port checkpoint at just 19 m/s: far below the 45–52 m/s superelevation estimates and below our fitted 31. Three instruments, three answers — descent speed, splash-line speed, post-turn speed — is exactly the fingerprint a junction step leaves. The remaining verification needs the footage geometry: geolocating the cameras, orienting their view axes against the corridor map (§02), and feature-tracking the frames is now a top task (§13). It matters because the border speed is simultaneously the most-quoted number of the disaster and the one our model currently reproduces worst.

The up-valley wedge, measured (6 September). The hypothesis that a large volume ran up the Chinese arm rested on a contour trace (~3.5 km), Xinhua's "about 3 km" of damaged G216, and a Sentinel-2 change test cut in angular sectors, which averaged hillsides in with the valley floor. The ratio end-point does not move when the corridor half-width is changed to 100 or 250 m; the NDVI end-point does, between 1.25 and 2.5 km, and the 3.0–3.25 km bin is 92% cloud, so the honest statement is that the stripping is full-strength to 2 km, marginal from 2.25, and gone by 3.5. The control behaves as a through-flow should: the downstream Bhote Koshi corridor stays at 1.3–2.8× to the edge of the image at 5.25 km, and the Lhende at 3–6× over 6 km. So the stripped ground is there between the junction and the head of the contour — which is what distinguishes a backwater wedge from a contour that merely runs up a valley — and it ends where the contour trace and the road damage end. China's Ministry of Natural Resources' remote-sensing centre (Guo Zhaocheng, on CCTV, 28 August, from the Ministry's 3D imagery) says the debris flow "ran up the Gyirong Tsangpo against the current, affecting a distance of nearly 3 km"; every later official statement repeats that one reading. Independently of it, the road evidence clusters at the same distance: about 3 km of the G216 destroyed, the road cut about 3 km from the port, a police vehicle bogged in fresh mud 3 km from the port at about 11:00 on the day, and Resuo village, 3 km up, reconnected the next day. One outlier, a container driver's "about five kilometres" in the Kathmandu Post, is contradicted by tourists stopped unharmed at 5 km that morning. Read together, the surge went about 3 km up the valley toward Gyirong. A reading of the open sub-metre imagery (Planet's Pelican scenes of 27 August and 1 September, 0.55 m, released under CC-BY-NC) adds a wrinkle worth keeping: fresh grey fill floors the whole valley from the junction to at least 2.5 km, the reach from 2.5 to 3.5 km is cloud in every sub-metre scene, and from about 3.8 km, past the bend, a fill at roughly twice the pre-event channel width runs to a tributary junction at about 4.6 km along the channel, where the river returns to its old width. That fill is in both the 27 August and the 1 September scenes. Whether it is run-up of the surge or sedimentation in the reach that ponded behind the sediment plug at the junction — Chinese engineers found the bed raised nearly 5 m and water over 15 m deep in the last 1.7 km during the repair — the imagery cannot say, and the Sentinel-2 corridor test sees only a weak, single-bin signal there. Distances also shift by 0.2–0.3 km depending on whether they are measured from the river junction or the checkpoint, along the river or the road. Caveats that stay attached: both-epoch clear-sky coverage is 42.6% of the area and 8–100% by bin; the cloud mask does not remove terrain shadow; the centreline is the pre-event channel; and the gorge bed was already largely bare, which caps the ratio. The volume parked in that arm during passage — 10–29 Mm³ by the wedge geometry — remains a model quantity, not a measurement.

09Downstream into India

The wave did not stop at Devghat. At the Valmikinagar barrage on the Gandak (the Narayani's Indian name), roughly 90 river-kilometres further on, Bihar's control-room figures — transcribed in local press — show the arrival: from a ~2,900–3,100 m³/s base, discharge climbed from 20:30 IST to a flat peak of 4,253 m³/s (150,200 cusecs) at 23:00–23:30 IST, the highest figure recorded there that day, then receded overnight. All 36 gates were open. That is a lag of ~7¼ hours from the Devghat peak and ~27% attenuation — and a celerity of only ~3.4 m/s, half the hill-reach value, exactly what the circuit picture predicts when the line runs onto the Chitwan floodplain: a wide, flat, fat-capacitance section. (An earlier spike in the barrage record at 16:00 IST is almost certainly a gate-operation transient, not routed flow — it is simultaneous with the Devghat peak 90 km upstream.)

The 28 August barrier-lake overtopping in the headwaters — feared as a second flood — is not resolvable at the barrage: fluctuations stayed within ~±150 m³/s of normal. Consistent with the ladder's lesson: by 300 km, the RC cascade has low-pass-filtered small pulses into the noise. The fine multi-pulse structure, if it existed, lived in the reaches where the gauges were destroyed; the Galchhi record and India's CWC hourly archive are where it would still be written.

10Does it travel? The Chamoli stress test

Every fitted law flatters the event it was fitted to. If this pipeline is to be worth anything as a warning tool it has to travel — so we took it, frozen, to the most hostile documented case: Chamoli, 7 February 2021. Winter, a near-dry channel, a 26.9 Mm³ mass that was ~80% rock — the opposite of a monsoon river valley in every regime variable — and ground truth of rare quality (a seismic detachment time, video-tracked speeds at three points, a 34–37 minute arrival at Tapovan 23 km downstream; Shugar et al., Science). The Trishuli-fitted kinematic law, applied verbatim, fails exactly as regime thinking predicts: it delivers the front to Tapovan in 11 minutes — three times too fast — while passing all three local speed checks. A slope-only law has no flow regime and no event size; Chamoli is precisely the event that exposes both omissions.

So we routed the actual release block down the actual profile with the ladder’s momentum equation (§08) plus one new term: Coulomb basal friction, which turns the friction law into Voellmy’s — and collapses back to Manning (and to every published chart in this report, bit for bit) when μ = 0. The dissection came out clean. A constant μ fails in both directions at once: a hundred-metre-deep flow outruns Coulomb friction in the steep upper canyon, while the flat basin at Raini (slope ~0.015) is terminal — the front stalls there and never reaches Tapovan. And the observations themselves contain the diagnosis: the mean speed to Tapovan (~11 m/s) is below the slowest of the three local speeds (12 m/s) — arithmetic that forces an intermittent, stop-and-go front, surging when moving and stopped in between.

What closes the gap is Shugar et al.’s own mechanism, made dynamic. The ~20% ice melted progressively under frictional heating — their energy budget (and ours, §04) shows the fall’s energy was almost exactly sufficient to melt all of it — so the friction dial μ slides from a dry-granular value toward a watery one as the mass descends. Both endpoints exist in the literature untouched by Chamoli: Scheidegger’s 1973 volume–mobility law gives μ ≈ 0.30 for a dry rock avalanche of this volume, and the wet endpoint (~0.02) is this report’s own Manning limit. One physical constant remains: dissipated heat takes minutes to conduct into ice fragments before it melts them, so the melt state rides with the mass and lags the fall. That thermal lag τ is the one degree of freedom we fit, and the observed arrival selects τ ≈ 5 minutes — ice debris of ~6 cm, a plausible comminuted size. The rest is then out of sample: arrival 36.3 min against an observed 34–37 — inside the window rather than beside it; mean speed 10.6 m/s against ~11; the km-16 flow speed passes. The basin crossing is where it now reads worst: 13 minutes against the ~27 that an independent analysis (Rana et al.) published for that reach. That gap widened after we fixed an interpolation bug in the arrival calculation and re-ran — the arrival itself improved, the basin transit got worse, and both are reported here rather than only the first. The model still cannot do stick-slip — it smears the surging into steady creep, so the two local speeds nearest Tapovan read low — and we say so rather than fit harder: that microstructure needs friction hysteresis or two-phase mechanics (Shugar’s team needed full r.avaflow to do better than this).

Why this matters for warning The water-limit law is the earliest-arrival envelope: every gram of granular character only delays a front. A warning-tier map built on the water law therefore errs toward promising less time than a real mixed flow would allow — the conservative direction — and the regime dial Chamoli forced into the model is exactly the physics that says how much extra time a drier, rockier event grants. One equation family now spans both endmembers: μ → 0 is Langtang’s monsoon flood; μ(f) with thermal lag is Chamoli’s rock avalanche.

11The climate question

Much of the coverage frames this as a climate disaster. For this event, that framing claims more than the evidence holds — and so does its mirror image, "nothing to do with climate." The two claims need separating.

What the record does not support: this was not a weather event (a clear, rain-free morning), not a glacial-lake outburst, and — per the ledger above — not even mostly glacial water: melt was a ~8% term in the v1 ledger and, at the 14–34 Mm³ envelope of §00, at most 1.1–2.5 Mm³ — a few per cent. The proximate cause was a bedrock wedge failure of a kind steep, tectonically active Himalayan faces have produced throughout the Holocene; USGS notes an earlier event on the same massif in 2015 — that one off the south flank, 7 km away and into a different valley, so "the same mountain" rather than "the same location" (§02). And much of the death toll is an exposure story — a border post, a customs yard, and hundreds of hydropower workers from 13 projects packed into one corridor, with 38 minutes between collapse and SMS alert. No formal attribution study exists yet; any causal percentage quoted this week is ahead of the science.

What is genuinely climate-linked — mechanism, not hand-waving: the failure zone sits at ~5,200 m, in the elevation band where warming permafrost degrades the ice that cements jointed rock — the best-documented driver of increasing high-mountain rock-slope failure. Locally, glacierised areas above 4,000 m are warming ~0.31 °C per decade; Langtang's glaciers have retreated 1.5× faster since 2000 than before; Lirung itself is back 450 m since 1990, debuttressing the slopes it once held up. The warmest ground and lake temperatures in two years were observed in the weeks before failure, with meltwater filling crevasses — the same water-at-the-bed mechanism blamed for the 2016 Aru glacier collapses in Tibet. And the neighbouring headwater produced a supraglacial-lake outburst thirteen months earlier — one of the most directly warming-linked hazard types in the Himalaya. Two disasters in one corridor in thirteen months is anecdote, not statistics; it is also what the trend literature predicts.

The defensible one-liner Climate change did not cause this flood — but it is plausibly raising the base rate of the thing that triggered it, and nobody can yet say by how much. The water and the casualties are a story of monsoon rivers, valley geometry and exposure; the climate signal, if it is anywhere, is in the rock face at 5,200 metres, not in the wave.

12For the hydropower cascade

This corridor carried one of the densest hydropower cascades in the Himalaya: 13 projects — 748 MW built or building — took damage, 431 MW came off Nepal's grid, and the missing include on the order of nine hundred hydropower workers, from the 111 MW Rasuwagadhi plant at the border T-junction to the hundreds caught in Upper Trishuli-1's tunnel (~350 of whom were rescued alive on 28 August). Every one of these projects is run-of-river — small weirs, hours of pondage at most. There was no storage reservoir anywhere on the system capable of absorbing a surge (the basin's one large storage proposal, the 1,200 MW Budhi Gandaki, is unbuilt): in circuit terms the cascade added no meaningful capacitance to the line — only exposure. For readers in the industry, three things in this analysis translate directly:

— Up-valley, the wave outruns any human alert chain. The front covered the first 22 km in seven minutes forty; SMS alerts left 38 minutes after the collapse. Only automated, upstream-triggered protection — seismic or stage tripwires wired into plant control, tunnel egress alarms, intake gates that fail safe — operates on the timescale that matters in the upper reach. Below Betrawati the wave took hours: there the alert chain can work, and routing models of the kind in this report are the relevant planning tool.
— Confluences are exposure hot-spots. The junction step (§08) concentrates impact, backwater and momentum exchange exactly where cascade infrastructure tends to sit — at tributary mouths. Rasuwagadhi's headworks stood 400 m below one.
— Plant records are the missing instrument. Headworks stage sensors, gate logs and SCADA historians across the thirteen projects — including drowned plants whose servers survived — plausibly hold the only high-frequency hydrographs of the event's upper reach, better than anything the washed-out public gauges left behind. Recovering those records would do more for understanding this flood, and for protecting the next cascade, than any instrument that could be deployed now.
— The trigger can extend weeks earlier. Sentinel-1 InSAR shows the failed mass creeping ~10 mm/month and accelerating in the days before collapse (Shirzaei, Virginia Tech, via Nature news, 2 Sept) — a satellite watchlist tier above the seismic tripwire. Note that a second, independent reading of the same satellite record (geopera, 31 Aug) finds under two metres of steady motion in a hundred days and no acceleration at all; the two disagree, neither is peer-reviewed, and precursory creep should not be treated as established. And GFZ's Niels Hovius has said seismic-signal-based downstream warning was feasible "with only minutes' delay" — the automated-tier case, made independently.

— Confirmation of which river the Devghat gauge measures and its rating curve. DHM's Devghat station is very likely the combined Narayani below the Kali Gandaki confluence (base ~2,900 m³/s in late August, as the FFD's own numbers imply) — this single fact sets the observed excess peak the routing models must hit, and re-scores the scenario table.
— Evidence of a significant pre-event lake in the Chhochen headwater. Petley and Davies both say no GLOF; a pre-event Sentinel-2 lake inventory would close that loophole for good.
— Satellite confirmation of the reported brief damming of the Lhende Khola. ICIMOD proposes it; Hugh Sinclair objects that the 7-minute border arrival is too fast for damming to be primary. Our timing fit accommodates a minutes-scale impoundment but does not require one.
