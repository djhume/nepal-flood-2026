The Trishuli Investigation

On the morning of 26 August 2026 a mountainside above the Lhende Khola, in Rasuwa, fell into the gorge of a small river. Seven minutes and forty seconds later a front of ice, rock and mud, moving at about 170 km/h, destroyed the border crossing at Rasuwagadhi, 22 km downstream, met the main river there, and ran the length of the Bhote Koshi and the Trishuli as a flood. This project asks one narrow question with physics: where did all that water come from — because the answer decides what a warning system has to watch for.

As of 5 September, 1,342 people are confirmed dead in Nepal and 4,886 missing, with 31 dead and 531 missing on the Chinese side, and more than 5,300 injured. The missing count has begun to fall while the death toll rises — that is bodies being recovered, not people being found alive. Of roughly 900 recovered by 1 September, about 4% had been identified. Many of the dead were hydropower workers, from the plant at the border to the tunnels at Upper Trishuli-1. Every figure on this site is provisional.

The wave carried away bridges, vehicles, homes and people. No term in this arithmetic stands for them, and none of the numbers on these pages should be read as if it did.

दिवंगतहरूको स्मृतिमा — in memory of those lost.

What this is, before you read any of it. Independent, AI-assisted analysis published days after the event by one person working with a research assistant. It is not peer-reviewed, no Nepali scientist has read it, and by its own licence it is not fit to sit under an operational warning system, an evacuation decision or a hazard zoning without independent expert review. Five results have been withdrawn so far and all five are still readable below. The contribution is arithmetic on numbers already in public — the relative sizes of the terms — not a finding about how well anyone else has done their job.

What we found

Every finding carries a confidence chip, and the colour is the part that means something. green the conclusion survives every combination of inputs we can still defend. blue it has survived the tests run against it, including a named challenge, but it rests on at least one input that could still move. amber unresolved, under revision, or partly withdrawn. The chip says how hard a finding has been shot at, not how much we believe it.

01

The water was already in the riverholds across the range

Melting ice is expensive and falling is cheap: a kilogram falling 1,200 metres releases about a twenty-eighth of the heat needed to melt a kilogram of ice. At the collapse size our own evidence supports (finding 04) that caps frictional melt at 1.1 million cubic metres on best evidence and 2.5 million if every dial is turned in melt's favour at once — against the ~20 million cubic metres of "extra" water in the official estimate. That figure is Nepal DHM's Flood Forecasting Division, in their 27 August press release: discharge above base flow, integrated at Devghat over the 14:10–18:00 window. It is a measurement at the bottom of the river, 199 km from the collapse, and it is not itself a claim about meltwater — which is the point. Something has to supply it, and melting ice cannot. Melt reaches that official figure only under the largest published volume (200 Mm³, six times the top of our envelope), 80% ice, the full 4,000-metre drop and a heat-to-ice partition at the very top of the published range; that combination yields 20.7 Mm³, and every step down from it falls away fast. So the wave was mostly the monsoon-swollen river itself, swept up like snow before a plough and delivered all at once — from the border junction onward. Above it the Lhende held about half a million cubic metres of water against a collapse of 14–34 million, so the first 22 km were the mountain itself, not a flood (report §04b). An energy budget and a source-by-source ledger agree on the volume, and the routing models bracket the peak — three checks that share a channel profile, not three independent measurements. The melt ceiling quoted here is computed at the size envelope of finding 04, which is under revision and probably too small; a larger collapse raises the ceiling in proportion, and the conclusion holds until the envelope reaches roughly ten times its current top.

02

It did not fall like dry rockdry rock excluded

The border camera's clock — seven minutes forty — turns out to discriminate what the falling mass was made of, not how big it was. Dry-rock versions of the collapse arrive at 17 to 30 minutes no matter how large we make them; icy and wet versions arrive within a few minutes of the clock, inside the tolerance the test allows. Note which way that result points: it excludes dry rock, it does not establish slush. What supplied the mobility — ice in the falling mass, the wet moraine and 2025 flood deposits it ran over, or monsoon water in the valley — is unresolved (finding 06), though the run down the Lhende is what an ice-rich avalanche does on its own: the mass was mostly rock and ice when it reached the border, lubricated the way a skate is, and Kolka 2002 covered a similar distance in a similar time (report §04b, added 6 September). Published imagery agrees qualitatively: several groups describe bedrock failure that carried a hanging glacier, drone footage shows a white stream leading the brown dust cloud, and the border footage was described as "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks".

The clock itself was challenged this week. Another group put the border arrival at seventeen minutes — which is exactly what our dry-rock runs give, and would have falsified the finding. It survived, and on 6 September we stopped relying on anyone's account of the camera and read the footage itself: the station's own overlay is legible in the video, and it tracks real time exactly. That retires the awkward part of this argument, which was that the timestamp reached us only through a partisan outlet (the Epoch Times).

Reading it also corrected our own number. The destruction begins at 10:59:50 Beijing, which is 08:44:50 in Nepal — 7 min 40 s after the collapse, not the 6 min 50 s we and others had published. That figure is what you get reading the overlay to the minute and assuming zero seconds; the seconds are ~50. The average speed over the first 22 km is therefore 47.8 m/s, not 53.7. Slower, by 12%, and it makes no difference to this finding: seventeen minutes is still excluded, and so is twenty-three. What owes nothing to any camera is a Nepali gauge — Syabrubesi, km 37.6, on Nepali time, read 3.8 m and rising at 08:50 — and you cannot take 17 minutes to reach km 22 and 13 to reach km 37.6.

The other end of that interval has not had the same treatment. Every elapsed time on this site is measured from 08:37:10 Nepali time, which is the USGS catalogue origin for event us7000tbwb, reclassified from an M4.4 tectonic earthquake to an M5.2 landslide-generated signal (GFZ record Mw 5.7, "consistent with a landslide"). We audited the camera and took the catalogue on trust. A landslide radiates an emergent onset rather than the sharp first arrival a catalogue origin-time solution is built around, and no force-time inversion has been published that would pin it independently. Seconds there do not threaten the finding — 17 minutes is 460 seconds away — but they do move the speed, and the correction we made this morning was itself only 50 seconds. It is on the list.

03

The method travels — one test stands, one is withdrawnone of two stands

Against Chamoli 2021 the model reproduces the arrival time at Tapovan once frictional melting is given a thermal lag, with a single fitted number and the rest out of sample. It is a partial pass, not a clean one: inside the same run the Raini basin transit comes out at 13 minutes against the ~27 an independent analysis published for that reach, and that gap widened when we fixed a bug in the arrival calculation — the arrival improved and the basin got worse. Both are in the report. A second test, against the 2012 Seti River flood, was reported here as a clean pass; it has been withdrawn. The channel profile that test ran on was built by a routine that filled gaps in the map data with straight lines across a mountain rim, and the repair applied to those bad elevations flattened 31 of the 54 km to zero gradient. On a channel that really has a slope, the same model arrives four times too early. Both of that test's conclusions — the timing pass and the inference that a stored lake was required — are off the table until the event is rebuilt.

That post-mortem ended with a fix — "a profile-integrity check belongs in every path build" — which then stayed a sentence for three days. It is now a script that runs over every channel this project uses and fails the build rather than warning. Seti as published fails all four of its gates. The Trishuli and Chamoli channels pass. And one result worth stating plainly: the repaired Seti channel still fails, on a 972-metre upward step at km 8, which is why that rebuild is a rebuild and not a patch.

04

How big the collapse was — still being worked on, and our own number is moving upwe are still working on this

This finding used to say "the collapse was far smaller than the headline figures". We are no longer confident of that, and we would rather say so at the top than leave it in the wording and hedge it further down. The envelope below is what our ensemble still produces and we have not withdrawn it — but four separate lines of evidence, three of them found on 6 September, now lean larger, and none of them has been folded into the number yet. Treat the range as the current output of one model, not as our best estimate of the truth.

Published estimates of the fallen mass span a factor of four hundred, from half a million cubic metres to two hundred million. Rather than argue one estimate at a time, we asked which combinations of size and composition are consistent with everything measurable at once — the clock, the border speed, the valley floor the flood scoured out, the debris it left — and ran the model across the whole disputed range, keeping only what passed every test. The runs that pass put it at 14–34 million cubic metres (median 21), an order of magnitude below the largest published figures. Changing our shakiest input by a factor of 2.5 moved that answer by a third, so it is the measurements driving it. Size is the only thing the passing runs agree on; how wet or icy it was is not resolved. One input needs flagging: the border speed we scored against is the 45–52 m/s superelevation estimate, and the first peer-reviewed study of the event — a frame-by-frame video analysis published 1 September — measures 19 m/s at the same place. That re-score has now been run, and the answer is sharper than a moved envelope: scored against 19 m/s, nothing survives at all — 0 samples of 200, against 21 of 200 for the front speed. And the incompatibility has nothing to do with size. No sample anywhere in the 1–200 Mm³ range satisfies the border arrival and a 19 m/s border speed together; nor the Syabrubesi arrival and 19 m/s. Each is reachable alone — 64 runs make the clock, 35 make the speed — never the same run. Read that carefully, because it is not proof we are right: it says these two numbers cannot describe the same thing in this model, which is what we have argued (19 m/s is the post-turn water surface, not the front, and would need an impossible run-up coefficient of 2.72 to raise the observed cliff mark), but it is equally consistent with our model's own speed-to-arrival relationship being wrong. Both scorings are published.

A correction found in the same audit, and it is one we should not have needed to find. This morning's clock correction — 6 min 50 s to 7 min 40 s — updated every place a human reads that number and no place the model does. Six code files were still scoring against 7.0 minutes, so this envelope had been computed against a figure the site had already retracted. Fixed and rerun. It barely moves the answer — 13.3–34.0 Mm³, median 23.2, against the 14–34 and median 21 published — which is a relief and not the lesson. The lesson is that correcting a published number is not done when the prose reads right. A second exposure, added late on 6 September: a first estimate of the volume that ran up the Chinese arm, divided by the share of the flow that went that way, straddles this envelope: inside it at one corner, at its top at the next, well above it elsewhere. The share is unmeasured and the valley geometry cannot fix it. And a mud line at the Upper Trishuli-1 headworks, 21 km below the junction, stands roughly 45–70 m above the pre-event bed where the runs that pass this envelope put 5–21 m of flow. Two routes that share none of the envelope's inputs both say it is probably too small. The envelope was never scored on a depth; the next version will be. Preliminary, hours old, and set out in full in report §12b; the number above stands with this caveat attached, and so does the melt ceiling in finding 01 that depends on it.

05

We proved our own model wrong, then fixed itfalsified, then fixed

The model could drop sediment but never pick any up. Adding erosion — using formulas published for other rivers on other continents, with nothing tuned to this event — produced 3.8 million cubic metres of scour against 3.2 measured by satellite stereo over the 45% of the corridor it could see. But it also broke the model: with deposition working properly, the flood stalled before reaching the towns. So we pushed it until it broke — 150 runs across every plausible combination — and none could arrive on time and leave the right debris. That falsified the shape of the model, not its numbers. The fix was to stop treating "sediment" as one substance: boulders settle and grind, silt and ice ride with the water and leave no measurable deposit. An independent analyst — Darcy Weedman, writing at geopera — was driven to the same conclusion the same week by a completely different method, machine-learning calibration of a 2D morphodynamic model against stereo satellite imagery, which pulled the settling velocity down by a factor of three. Two unreviewed analyses agreeing is weaker evidence than two peer-reviewed ones, and we would rather name the weight than borrow it. Name it properly, though, and it is weaker still: "a completely different method" is true of the solver and not of the inputs. Both analyses read the same public stereo imagery, the same week's literature and the same handful of published estimates, and geopera is also fast, unreviewed analysis of this event. Different solvers over shared inputs are not two independent measurements. This is also the source we lean on hardest elsewhere — the erosion figure and the border superelevation that finding 04 scores against are both theirs — and one of their numbers has already been retracted and corrected in our scoring.

06

What is not settledopen

What the mass was made of is not resolved — wetness and ice content span nearly the whole range we allowed, and an earlier version of this analysis appeared to settle them, which turned out to be an artefact of a constraint that was too strict. Our model also puts the main debris deposit in the wrong place: satellite stereo shows it 40–43 km downstream and ours drops it too early — geopera's model got that right and ours did not. A final out-of-sample test half failed. The peak discharge at Devghat met its pass criterion — within a factor of two — for every passing version, but all 26 land below the observation (1,752–2,889 m³/s against ~2,900), so "passed" there means "not contradicted", not "reproduced". The river height at Galchhi came out at about 3.6 m against roughly 9 observed.

That last one we chased down late on 6 September, because the obvious suspect was the channel itself — the same component whose failure withdrew the Seti test — and the profile check built for finding 03 says it is not the elevations. The lower river is genuinely so flat that its true fall per 400-metre sample, about 0.6 m, is smaller than the elevation data's own noise, so the monotone-descent step every model here applies turns a real gentle slope into a staircase: 82% of the last 39 km ends up at zero gradient. That looks fatal and is not, because the smoothing applied afterwards averages it back out — the profile the model actually runs on is 1.4% flat and holds the reach-average slopes to within 10% of the raw data. So the elevations are cleared, and the Galchhi miss points instead at the other half of the channel: the widths, which below km 60 are not measurements at all but a hydraulic-geometry rule of thumb fitted to discharge. Naming a suspect is not fixing it — but both failures at the bottom of the river, Galchhi's height and Devghat's one-sided residual, now point at the same un-measured input, and so does the Hakubesi mud line 65 km further up.

7 September 2026 — midday The size envelope was re-run against the mud-line map, and nothing passes — which is the most useful result this project has had. Two hundred releases from one to two hundred million cubic metres, scored on the corridor's mapped water depths for the first time, with the measured channel widths, the Chinese arm as the pond the map found, the corrected clock and a speed compared like with like. Not one satisfies the depths and the border clock together, at any size. The runs deep enough to leave the mud lines are 86–142 million cubic metres, three to five times the envelope on this page — and every one of them reaches the border in 4.2–4.8 minutes at 45–62 m/s where the record says 7 min 40 s and about 34. The runs that make the clock never put more than 31 m of water in a gorge whose mud lines stand at 60 to 100. Roughness does not bridge it: at seventy metres of depth the model's friction all but vanishes and a big release runs as a water bore at the Froude limit, while the real flow was that deep and half as fast. So the structure is wrong, not the numbers: a debris flow this deep carries a resistance the composition dial does not have. The measured widths, for the record, are one and a half to three times wider than the rule of thumb, which kills the long-standing excuse for the low stages. 6 September 2026 — late A new, loose line of evidence on the collapse size, and it leans larger. Dave Hume's method: treat the border junction as a node, measure the volume that ran up the Chinese arm from the valley floor and the imagery, split it at the node by the valley geometry, and subtract what the Lhende contributed. On the Copernicus 30 m DEM the arm held somewhere between 13 and 70 million cubic metres at peak depending on how the mud-line head is read. The share that went up the arm is the crux, and the valley geometry cannot fix it: the Lhende's last 500 metres bend toward the downstream exit, the kilometre above runs at right angles to both exits, and a 100-metre-deep flow at 170 km/h does not follow a 500-metre bend. Anywhere from a tenth to a half is defensible. Divide one by the other and the corners straddle finding 04's envelope: one lands inside it, the next at its top, and most well above. It is a first pass with an unmeasured split and a 30-metre DEM, so it cannot move the finding, but it leans larger and it is recorded here rather than held back. What would fix the split is peak-discharge continuity at the junction — mud lines and superelevation just above and just below it, which helicopter footage can supply — and the tool for that is built. Working in the dossier (§17); what would tighten it is listed in report §13.

Also corrected: this site claimed the contact stress between ice grains collapses the melting point by about 70 °C. It does not. That was a straight-line extrapolation past the point where ordinary ice stops existing; the real floor is about 22 °C, and beyond it the high-pressure forms of ice melt hotter, not colder. The mechanism is premelting — a liquid-like film that is there without being squeezed — which is why you can skate at −30 °C. The conclusion it supported is unchanged.

5 September 2026 — evening A cold read of the whole site, and the corrections it produced. Three of them matter. The melt ceiling was quoted as sitting "far below" the official 20 Mm³ while this site's own chart showed 15 against 20; carrying finding 04's size envelope into the energy budget — which should have happened the moment that envelope existed — puts the real ceiling at 1.1–2.5 Mm³ and makes the claim both true and much stronger. A bar on the plain-English chart labelled "river already lying in the channel, 47 Mm³" was the total wave at Devghat; the standing channel water is 22.4. And the border-clock resolution was presented with more confidence than its sources carry: the decisive overlay quote comes from a partisan outlet and Kargel's matching figure has no stated method, both of which are now on the page. Also: the published workings had fallen three days behind the findings and have been rebuilt; every page now says who geopera is; and the human toll is at the top of this page, where it should have been from the first version.

A rule, adopted here rather than just intended: nothing reaches this findings list on the day it is computed. New results go in this changelog first and move up only after they have survived a night and one out-of-sample check. Findings 04 and 05 were promoted the same day they were produced, which is how finding 03 got promoted and then withdrawn.
5 September 2026 Two new findings. The collapse was probably 14–34 million cubic metres, an order of magnitude below the largest published estimates. And the model was proved structurally wrong before it was fixed — no combination of inputs could satisfy the timing and the debris budget together until sediment was split into coarse and fine. A challenge to the border clock was chased down and resolved in the camera's favour. The Nepali translation was withdrawn pending review by a Nepali speaker.
3 September 2026 — night The Seti 2012 result is withdrawn. Building the missing erosion physics meant looking hard at channel gradients, and that exposed a bad channel profile underneath the Seti test: straight lines flown across a mountain rim, then flattened to zero slope by the routine meant to clean them. Rerun on a corrected channel, the test fails, and its second conclusion reverses. Nothing about the Trishuli findings depends on it. Meanwhile the erosion term now exists, and it puts the amount of material the flood tore out of the Langtang corridor within range of what satellite stereo actually measured — while saying the corridor cannot have received anything like the amount of rock the largest source estimates imply.
3 September 2026 — evening A deposition figure that geopera had themselves retracted was corrected in our scoring, which resolved a tension between the timing and deposit evidence — both now point the same way.
3 September 2026 The Chamoli 2021 timing gap closed by adding thermal lag to the melt physics. The corridor map was redrawn with automatic label placement.
2 September 2026 First public version: the water budget, the snowplow model and the river-as-circuit routing model.

What happens next

Two jobs, both named independently by the two sessions that worked on this on 6 September. The first is done as of the morning of 7 September: a map of every mud line in the corridor — the stripped-ground boundary extracted from the post-event imagery along both banks, the 8 m elevation model sampled under it, speeds from the inner–outer bank difference at bends, and a peak-stage and discharge profile for the whole upper corridor. It was gated on the three mud lines at the border junction and on the Hakubesi line, and it returned them (entry above; dossier §19; output/trimlines.csv). These are the water depths this analysis has never been scored against. Second, and next, the ensemble re-run with those depths as observables, the Chinese arm modelled as a backwater pond at the level the map found, the corrected clock and a properly specified border speed. That run is what turns "our number is moving up" into a number. Results go to the changelog first, as the rule above requires.

What would help most

  • The full Galchhi and Devghat gauge records (Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology, DHM) — including which station and rating curve the Devghat figures come from. This is the single dataset that would most sharpen every number here.
  • Hydropower plant records — headworks stage sensors, gate logs and SCADA historians across the thirteen affected projects, including plants that were damaged but whose servers or off-site replicas survived. These plausibly hold the only high-frequency measurements of the flood's upper reach, where the public gauges were destroyed.
  • Clear-sky stereo imagery or drone photogrammetry of the glacier below the collapse scar. No such measurement has been published, and it would directly test where the falling mass got its mobility.
  • India's CWC hourly archive for the Gandak stations, to extend the flood's routing beyond the border.
