The Trishuli Investigation

On the morning of 26 August 2026 a mountainside above the Lhende Khola, in Rasuwa, fell into the gorge of a small river. Seven minutes and forty seconds later a front of ice, rock and mud, moving at about 170 km/h, destroyed the border crossing at Rasuwagadhi, 22 km downstream, met the main river there, and ran the length of the Bhote Koshi and the Trishuli as a flood. This project asks one narrow question with physics: where did all that water come from — because the answer decides what a warning system has to watch for.

As of 5 September, 1,342 people are confirmed dead in Nepal and 4,886 missing, with 31 dead and 531 missing on the Chinese side, and more than 5,300 injured. The missing count has begun to fall while the death toll rises — that is bodies being recovered, not people being found alive. Of roughly 900 recovered by 1 September, about 4% had been identified. Many of the dead were hydropower workers, from the plant at the border to the tunnels at Upper Trishuli-1. Every figure on this site is provisional.

The wave carried away bridges, vehicles, homes and people. No term in this arithmetic stands for them, and none of the numbers on these pages should be read as if it did.

What we found

01

The water was already in the riverholds across the range

Melting ice is expensive and falling is cheap: a kilogram falling 1,200 metres releases about a twenty-eighth of the heat needed to melt a kilogram of ice. At the collapse size our own evidence supports (finding 04) that caps frictional melt at 1.1 million cubic metres on best evidence and 2.5 million if every dial is turned in melt's favour at once — against the ~20 million cubic metres of "extra" water officially estimated. Melt reaches that official figure only under the largest published volume (200 Mm³, six times the top of our envelope), 80% ice, the full 4,000-metre drop and a heat-to-ice partition at the very top of the published range; that combination yields 20.7 Mm³, and every step down from it falls away fast. So the wave was mostly the monsoon-swollen river itself, swept up like snow before a plough and delivered all at once — from the border junction onward. Above it the Lhende held about half a million cubic metres of water against a collapse of 14–34 million, so the first 22 km were the mountain itself, not a flood (report §04b). An energy budget and a source-by-source ledger agree on the volume, and the routing models bracket the peak — three checks that share a channel profile, not three independent measurements.

02

It did not fall like dry rockdry rock excluded

The border camera's clock — seven minutes forty — turns out to discriminate what the falling mass was made of, not how big it was. Dry-rock versions of the collapse arrive at 17 to 30 minutes no matter how large we make them; icy and wet versions arrive within a few minutes of the clock, inside the tolerance the test allows. Note which way that result points: it excludes dry rock, it does not establish slush. What supplied the mobility — ice in the falling mass, the wet moraine and 2025 flood deposits it ran over, or monsoon water in the valley — is unresolved (finding 06), though the run down the Lhende is what an ice-rich avalanche does on its own: the mass was mostly rock and ice when it reached the border, lubricated the way a skate is, and Kolka 2002 covered a similar distance in a similar time (report §04b, added 6 September). Published imagery agrees qualitatively: several groups describe bedrock failure that carried a hanging glacier, drone footage shows a white stream leading the brown dust cloud, and the border footage was described as "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks".

The clock itself was challenged this week. Another group put the border arrival at seventeen minutes — which is exactly what our dry-rock runs give, and would have falsified the finding. It survived, and on 6 September we stopped relying on anyone's account of the camera and read the footage itself: the station's own overlay is legible in the video, and it tracks real time exactly. That retires the awkward part of this argument, which was that the timestamp reached us only through a partisan outlet (the Epoch Times).

Reading it also corrected our own number. The destruction begins at 10:59:50 Beijing, which is 08:44:50 in Nepal — 7 min 40 s after the collapse, not the 6 min 50 s we and others had published. That figure is what you get reading the overlay to the minute and assuming zero seconds; the seconds are ~50. The average speed over the first 22 km is therefore 47.8 m/s, not 53.7. Slower, by 12%, and it makes no difference to this finding: seventeen minutes is still excluded, and so is twenty-three. What owes nothing to any camera is a Nepali gauge — Syabrubesi, km 37.6, on Nepali time, read 3.8 m and rising at 08:50 — and you cannot take 17 minutes to reach km 22 and 13 to reach km 37.6.

03

The method travels — one test stands, one is withdrawnone of two stands

Against Chamoli 2021 the model reproduces the arrival time at Tapovan once frictional melting is given a thermal lag, with a single fitted number and the rest out of sample. It is a partial pass, not a clean one: inside the same run the Raini basin transit comes out at 13 minutes against the ~27 an independent analysis published for that reach, and that gap widened when we fixed a bug in the arrival calculation — the arrival improved and the basin got worse. Both are in the report. A second test, against the 2012 Seti River flood, was reported here as a clean pass; it has been withdrawn. The channel profile that test ran on was built by a routine that filled gaps in the map data with straight lines across a mountain rim, and the repair applied to those bad elevations flattened 31 of the 54 km to zero gradient. On a channel that really has a slope, the same model arrives four times too early. Both of that test's conclusions — the timing pass and the inference that a stored lake was required — are off the table until the event is rebuilt.

04

The collapse was far smaller than the headline figuresenvelope only

Published estimates of the fallen mass span a factor of four hundred, from half a million cubic metres to two hundred million. Rather than argue one estimate at a time, we asked which combinations of size and composition are consistent with everything measurable at once — the clock, the border speed, the valley floor the flood scoured out, the debris it left — and ran the model across the whole disputed range, keeping only what passed every test. The runs that pass put it at 14–34 million cubic metres (median 21), an order of magnitude below the largest published figures. Changing our shakiest input by a factor of 2.5 moved that answer by a third, so it is the measurements driving it. Size is the only thing the passing runs agree on; how wet or icy it was is not resolved. One input needs flagging: the border speed we scored against is the 45–52 m/s superelevation estimate, and the first peer-reviewed study of the event — a frame-by-frame video analysis published 1 September — measures 19 m/s at the same place. We have not yet re-scored against it. If that number is the right one, this envelope moves.

05

We proved our own model wrong, then fixed itfalsified, then fixed

The model could drop sediment but never pick any up. Adding erosion — using formulas published for other rivers on other continents, with nothing tuned to this event — produced 3.8 million cubic metres of scour against 3.2 measured by satellite stereo over the 45% of the corridor it could see. But it also broke the model: with deposition working properly, the flood stalled before reaching the towns. So we pushed it until it broke — 150 runs across every plausible combination — and none could arrive on time and leave the right debris. That falsified the shape of the model, not its numbers. The fix was to stop treating "sediment" as one substance: boulders settle and grind, silt and ice ride with the water and leave no measurable deposit. An independent analyst — Darcy Weedman, writing at geopera — was driven to the same conclusion the same week by a completely different method, machine-learning calibration of a 2D morphodynamic model against stereo satellite imagery, which pulled the settling velocity down by a factor of three. Two unreviewed analyses agreeing is weaker evidence than two peer-reviewed ones, and we would rather name the weight than borrow it.

06

What is not settledopen

What the mass was made of is not resolved — wetness and ice content span nearly the whole range we allowed, and an earlier version of this analysis appeared to settle them, which turned out to be an artefact of a constraint that was too strict. Our model also puts the main debris deposit in the wrong place: satellite stereo shows it 40–43 km downstream and ours drops it too early — geopera's model got that right and ours did not. A final out-of-sample test half failed. The peak discharge at Devghat met its pass criterion — within a factor of two — for every passing version, but all 26 land below the observation (1,752–2,889 m³/s against ~2,900), so "passed" there means "not contradicted", not "reproduced". The river height at Galchhi came out at about 3.6 m against roughly 9 observed, which points at our estimate of the lower river's shape. And this is fast, independent, AI-assisted analysis published days after the event: it is not peer-reviewed, and no Nepali scientist has yet read it.

Our contribution is quantification, not correction. The scientists working on this event — Kargel, Shugar, Petley, ICIMOD and others — have consistently described river water and entrained sediment in their accounts of the flood. What nobody had published was the arithmetic: the relative sizes of the terms. That is the gap this project set out to fill, and where our numbers disagree with an agency's, both are shown. Nothing here is a finding about how well anyone else has done their job, and the warning-system section of the plain-English page is about a gap in the design of national systems generally, not about the people who were operating one that morning while four of its gauges were being destroyed.

What changed recently

6 September 2026 — evening A second cold read, an account of the event, and a rethink of the first 22 kilometres. The technical report now opens with a description of what happened — what fell, from where, onto what, what the border camera saw, where the deposits are, what the mud lines record, and what nobody yet knows — and the plain-English page carries the same account in plain words. The video measurement listed below is written up in full on both pages: the clock, the two-route camera calibration, the two sight lines 50 m apart that the front crossed in 1.066 s, the error budget and what failed, with a plan-view diagram of the geometry.

The up-valley question has an answer. Re-measuring the Sentinel-2 change along the river centreline instead of in wedges, the ground stripped up the Chinese arm fades out between about 2 and 3.5 km from the junction, while the two arms the flood flowed through stay stripped for the whole 5–8 km in view. With Xinhua's "about 3 km" of damaged highway the 3.5 km contour trace, and — found in tonight's literature check — China's Ministry of Natural Resources' own statement that part of the flow ran "nearly 3 km upstream in the Gyirong Tsangpo", four lines now put the surge about 3 km up the valley toward Gyirong. Sub-metre imagery shows fresh sediment to about 4.6 km, which may be ponding behind the junction plug rather than the surge; the report keeps both. Report §08.

Dave Hume asked whether the first 22 km carried much water at all. They did not: the Lhende held about half a million cubic metres against a collapse of 14–34 million, so what struck the border was at least four-fifths mountain — a rock–ice avalanche, not yet a flood. The run down the Lhende is what an ice-rich mass does on its own, and the physics is the physics of an ice skate: a premelted film at rest, and at avalanche speeds frictional melting that renews a lubricating film at millimetres per second for a few per cent of the fall's energy — lubrication, not water production. Kolka–Karmadon 2002 covered a similar distance in a similar time. This is written up as report §04b and on the plain page; it reframes finding 02 without moving it, and it locates finding 01 below the junction, where the 22 Mm³ of river was. Not a scored result, so it is here and not in the findings.

Corrections from the cold read: §01 of the report still said "six minutes and fifty seconds"; the plain page's cliff box still compared the mud-line speed with the old 52 m/s clock and called it a match "within a few per cent" (it is within ~15% of the corrected 47.8); its opening still carried a toll ten days old; "only something running like slush" and "wall of water" both claimed more than the evidence shows; the version numbers disagreed between pages. And the word "survivors", which this site had been using for the model runs that pass its tests, is gone. It should never have been used that way here.
6 September 2026 We stopped taking anyone's word for the border camera, and it cost us a number. Until today the timestamp that the composition finding rests on reached us only through a partisan outlet's account of what the overlay said. We obtained the footage and read it directly: the station's own clock is legible and tracks real time. That retires the awkward part of the argument — and corrects our own figure. The destruction begins at 10:59:50 Beijing, 08:44:50 in Nepal, which is 7 min 40 s after the collapse and not the 6 min 50 s we had published. Ours came from reading the overlay to the minute and assuming zero seconds. The 22-kilometre average front speed drops from 53.7 to 47.8 m/s — 12% slower. The finding stands: seventeen and twenty-three minutes are still excluded, the corrected 7 min 40 s lies inside the ±30% tolerance the composition test used, and the Syabrubesi gauge still carries the argument without any camera at all. The runs themselves have not yet been repeated at the corrected clock.

Also corrected: this site claimed the contact stress between ice grains collapses the melting point by about 70 °C. It does not. That was a straight-line extrapolation past the point where ordinary ice stops existing; the real floor is about 22 °C, and beyond it the high-pressure forms of ice melt hotter, not colder. The mechanism is premelting — a liquid-like film that is there without being squeezed — which is why you can skate at −30 °C. The conclusion it supported is unchanged.

Not promoted to the findings above, per the rule below: a first direct measurement of the flood front's speed at the border, taken off the video against two surveyed sight lines. 47 m/s, envelope 41–63. It agrees with the corrected clock and with geopera's superelevation, and it excludes the one published figure for the post-turn water — which is evidence that those two numbers were never measuring the same thing. It is a day old and needs checking; the method is now written up in report §03 and on the plain page.

What would help most

  • The full Galchhi and Devghat gauge records (Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology, DHM) — including which station and rating curve the Devghat figures come from. This is the single dataset that would most sharpen every number here.
  • Hydropower plant records — headworks stage sensors, gate logs and SCADA historians across the thirteen affected projects, including plants that were damaged but whose servers or off-site replicas survived. These plausibly hold the only high-frequency measurements of the flood's upper reach, where the public gauges were destroyed.
  • Clear-sky stereo imagery or drone photogrammetry of the glacier below the collapse scar. No such measurement has been published, and it would directly test where the falling mass got its mobility.
  • India's CWC hourly archive for the Gandak stations, to extend the flood's routing beyond the border.
