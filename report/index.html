The Trishuli Investigation

A mountainside fell into a Nepali river valley and, seven minutes later, a flood crossed the Chinese border 22 km downstream. This project asks a narrow question with physics: where did all that water actually come from — and what the answer means for warning people next time.

What we found

01

The water was already in the riverwell supported

A 1,200-metre fall cannot melt enough ice to make this flood — the energy ceiling sits far below the ~20 million cubic metres of "extra" water officially estimated. The wave was mostly the monsoon-swollen Trishuli itself, swept up like snow before a plough and delivered all at once. Three independent routes agree: an energy budget, a source-by-source ledger, and the routing model's own dynamics.

02

It fell like slush, not gravelrobust, one week old

The border camera's seven-minute clock turns out to discriminate what the falling mass was made of, not how big it was. Dry-rock versions of the collapse arrive late no matter how large we make them; icy and wet versions arrive on time. Published imagery agrees qualitatively — several groups describe bedrock failure that carried a hanging glacier, and drone footage shows a white stream leading the brown dust cloud. That seven-minute clock was itself challenged this week — another group put the arrival at seventeen minutes, which would have killed the finding — and it survived: the border camera's overlay reads 10:59 Beijing time, which is 08:44 in Nepal, matching Kargel's independently published figure to the second. The same footage was described as "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks". Still open: whether the lubricant came from ice in the falling mass, from the glacier beneath it, or from monsoon water in the valley.

03

The method travels — one test stands, one is withdrawnrevised 3 Sept

Against Chamoli 2021 the model reproduces the arrival time at Tapovan once frictional melting is given a thermal lag, with a single fitted number and the rest out of sample. A second test, against the 2012 Seti River flood, was reported here as a clean pass; it has been withdrawn. The channel profile that test ran on was built by a routine that filled gaps in the map data with straight lines across a mountain rim, and the repair applied to those bad elevations flattened 31 of the 54 km to zero gradient. On a channel that really has a slope, the same model arrives four times too early. Both of that test's conclusions — the timing pass and the inference that a stored lake was required — are off the table until the event is rebuilt.

04

The collapse was far smaller than the headline figuresnew, 5 Sept

Published estimates of the fallen mass span a factor of four hundred, from half a million cubic metres to two hundred million. Rather than argue one estimate at a time, we asked which combinations of size and composition are consistent with everything measurable at once — the clock, the border speed, the valley floor the flood scoured out, the debris it left — and ran the model across the whole disputed range, keeping only what passed every test. The survivors put it at 14–34 million cubic metres, an order of magnitude below the largest published figures. Changing our shakiest input by a factor of 2.5 moved that answer by a third, so it is the measurements driving it. Size is the only thing the survivors agree on; how wet or icy it was is not resolved.

05

We proved our own model wrong, then fixed itnew, 5 Sept

The model could drop sediment but never pick any up. Adding erosion — using formulas published for other rivers on other continents, with nothing tuned to this event — produced 3.8 million cubic metres of scour against 3.2 measured by satellite stereo. But it also broke the model: with deposition working properly, the flood died before reaching the towns. So we tested it to destruction — 150 runs across every plausible combination — and none could arrive on time and leave the right debris. That falsified the shape of the model, not its numbers. The fix was to stop treating "sediment" as one substance: boulders settle and grind, silt and ice ride with the water and leave no measurable deposit. Another group reached the same conclusion the same week by a completely different method.

06

What is not settledopen

What the mass was made of is not resolved — wetness and ice content span nearly the whole range we allowed, and an earlier version of this analysis appeared to settle them, which turned out to be an artefact of a constraint that was too strict. Our model also puts the main debris deposit in the wrong place: satellite stereo shows it 40–43 km downstream and ours drops it too early. A final out-of-sample test half failed — the flood volume at Devghat came out right for every surviving version, but the river height at Galchhi came out at about 3.6 m against roughly 9 observed, which points at our estimate of the lower river's shape. And this is fast, independent, AI-assisted analysis published days after the event: it is not peer-reviewed.

Our contribution is quantification, not correction. The scientists working on this event — Kargel, Shugar, Petley, ICIMOD and others — have consistently described river water and entrained sediment in their accounts of the flood. What nobody had published was the arithmetic: the relative sizes of the terms. That is the gap this project set out to fill, and where our numbers disagree with an agency's, both are shown.

What changed recently

5 September 2026 Two new findings. The collapse was probably 14–34 million cubic metres, an order of magnitude below the largest published estimates. And the model was proved structurally wrong before it was fixed — no combination of inputs could satisfy the timing and the debris budget together until sediment was split into coarse and fine. A challenge to the seven-minute border clock was chased down and resolved in the camera's favour. The Nepali translation was withdrawn pending review by a Nepali speaker.
3 September 2026 — night The Seti 2012 result is withdrawn. Building the missing erosion physics meant looking hard at channel gradients, and that exposed a bad channel profile underneath the Seti test: straight lines flown across a mountain rim, then flattened to zero slope by the routine meant to clean them. Rerun on a corrected channel, the test fails, and its second conclusion reverses. Nothing about the Trishuli findings depends on it. Meanwhile the erosion term now exists, and it puts the amount of material the flood tore out of the Langtang corridor within range of what satellite stereo actually measured — while saying the corridor cannot have received anything like the amount of rock the largest source estimates imply.
3 September 2026 — evening A retracted deposition figure from another group was corrected in our scoring, which resolved a tension between the timing and deposit evidence — both now point the same way.
3 September 2026 The Chamoli 2021 timing gap closed by adding thermal lag to the melt physics. The corridor map was redrawn with automatic label placement.
2 September 2026 First public version: the water budget, the snowplow model and the river-as-circuit routing model.

What would help most

  • The full Galchhi and Devghat gauge records (Nepal DHM) — including which station and rating curve the Devghat figures come from. This is the single dataset that would most sharpen every number here.
  • Hydropower plant records — headworks stage sensors, gate logs and SCADA historians across the thirteen affected projects, including plants that were damaged but whose servers or off-site replicas survived. These plausibly hold the only high-frequency measurements of the flood's upper reach, where the public gauges were destroyed.
  • Clear-sky stereo imagery or drone photogrammetry of the glacier below the collapse scar. No such measurement has been published, and it would directly test where the falling mass got its mobility.
  • India's CWC hourly archive for the Gandak stations, to extend the flood's routing beyond the border.
