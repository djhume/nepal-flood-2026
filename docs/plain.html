The Water Was Already in the River

On 26 August 2026 a piece of a Himalayan mountain fell into a river valley in Nepal. Seven minutes later a wall of water and debris taller than a ten-storey building crossed the Chinese border, 22 kilometres away. This is a plain-English account of where all that water came from, how we tested the idea, what we got wrong along the way, and what it means for keeping people alive next time. The full technical version, with every number and every assumption on display, is here.

What happened

At 8:37 on a clear monsoon morning, seismometers felt a mountainside let go: part of Langtang Lirung, a 7,200-metre peak about 60 kilometres north of Kathmandu, collapsed into the gorge of a small river called the Lende Khola. At 8:44 — seven minutes later — a border security camera 22 kilometres downstream filmed the flood arriving. That is an average speed of about 190 km/h down a mountain gorge.

The wave then ran the length of the Trishuli River, one of the most densely developed hydropower corridors in the Himalaya. Thirteen power stations were damaged. As of 4 September the reported toll stood at 1,259 dead and 5,083 missing in Nepal, with 21 dead and 541 missing on the Chinese side — and the missing count had risen by roughly 1,200 in three days, so every figure here should be read as provisional. Many of the dead were hydropower workers. The first SMS warnings went out 38 minutes after the collapse. For everyone in the upper valley, the flood had already come and gone.

By four in the afternoon, at Devghat 168 river-kilometres away, the river crested at roughly four times its normal monsoon flow. Nepal's flood authority estimated the event pushed about 20 million cubic metres of "extra" water down the river — around 8,000 Olympic swimming pools — in a single afternoon.

The puzzle

A falling mountain is rock and ice. Rock doesn't flood. So where did 8,000 pools of water come from? The standard first guess after disasters like this is melted glacier ice — the fall grinds the ice up, friction heats it, and it melts into flood water.

We doubted that, for a reason you can check with high-school physics.

Falling a kilometre doesn't buy much melting

When something falls, its energy of height turns into heat. A fall of 1,200 metres — roughly what this mass fell before it hit the river — gives each kilogram of material only enough heat to melt about 40 grams of ice, and that's if every scrap of the heat goes into melting, which it never does. Even under generous assumptions, melted ice can account for only a few million cubic metres here. The official estimate of extra water is several times bigger than any defensible melting budget.

So most of the flood's water was not made by the mountain. It was already in the valley.

The thing that surprises everyone Warming ice is nearly free. Melting it is enormously expensive. Raising a kilogram of ice by one degree takes about 2,100 joules; actually melting that kilogram takes 334,000 — you could warm ice by 159 degrees for the price of melting it once. So it makes almost no difference whether the glacier started at −20 °C or −0.1 °C: the two differ by 6% in what it costs to turn them into water. That is why this argument is robust even though nobody has ever measured that glacier's temperature. And pressure doesn't rescue it either — squeezing ice does lower its melting point, but only by about 0.0074 °C per atmosphere, and the effect is reversible. Release the pressure and it freezes straight back. Pressure moves the phase boundary; it doesn't pay the bill.

The snowplow

Picture the monsoon-swollen river as a long line of water lying in the valley, already flowing. Now send a fast, heavy wave down it. The wave acts like a snowplow: its front overruns the slower river water ahead of it, sweeps it up, and pushes it along. Kilometre after kilometre, the wave grows by collecting river that was already there.

When we do the arithmetic along the whole 168 km, roughly three-quarters of the water that passed the downstream gauge was ordinary river water, gathered up and delivered all at once. The mountain provided the push; the monsoon provided the water. Other Himalayan and Andean disasters, when examined closely, have told the same story.

This distinction is not academic. If flood volume comes mostly from the valley's own river, then the size of a future flood depends on the season — the same collapse in February and in August produce utterly different disasters. Warning systems should know that.

It fell like slush, not gravel

Remember the border camera: 22 km in seven minutes. We simulated the collapse many different ways — small and large, dry rock, icy, wet — and asked which versions could reach that camera on time. The result was stark. Every dry-rock version arrived late — 17 to 30 minutes, never seven — no matter how big we made it. Every icy or wet version arrived on time.

The physics is intuitive once said aloud: dry broken rock fights itself. Every fragment grinds against its neighbours, burning off the energy of the fall — like a truckload of gravel dumped down a slope, violent but self-braking. Ice and water lubricate. A wet or icy mass keeps the fall's energy as speed. Only something running like slush covers that gorge in seven minutes.

There is a second reason ice is slippery, and it is not melting. Where two ice grains touch, the contact stress is enormous — of order 10,000 atmospheres — and there the melting point really does collapse, by perhaps 70 °C, and a microscopic film of water forms. That film is why ice is slippery underfoot and why an icy landslide can run fast without melting much of itself at all. Lubrication, not production.

The seven minutes nearly came apart

That whole finding rests on one number: seven minutes. So when another group — geopera, doing careful satellite work and publishing openly — put the border arrival at seventeen minutes, it was serious. Our dry-rock simulations arrive at seventeen minutes exactly. If they were right, our finding was dead.

Chasing it down took a day and turned into a small detective story. They never mention the camera at all: their method is openly licensed satellite data, and the camera footage had been actively censored — two clips of the border gate going down were blocked on Chinese platforms, even inside private one-to-one messages. It wasn't available to them. Then a fact-check muddied it further by noting the video's on-screen clock runs on Chinese time, two and a quarter hours ahead of Nepal's.

The resolution was sitting in Chinese-language reporting: the footage is stamped 10:59 Beijing time, which is 8:44 in Nepal. The conversion had already been done correctly. And 8:44 minus the 8:37:10 collapse is 6 minutes 50 seconds — which is, to the second, the figure the glaciologist Jeffrey Kargel published independently. A Nepali river gauge 37 km downstream, running on Nepali time and owing nothing to any camera, agrees: it cannot have taken seventeen minutes to reach a point the flood had already passed.

The same Chinese description of the footage gave us something we had been hunting for weeks. The wave that hit the border was "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks". Ice, visible, 22 kilometres from the mountain — at exactly the place where the timing argument is made.

Reading a flood off a cliff

Floods leave mud lines. The trick is knowing what a given line means, because a line's height depends on where it is.

On a sheltered wall, the flow slides past and the mud line sits near the real water surface. But where a flow drives head-on into a cliff, it stops dead and converts all its speed into height — the same way water climbs the far bank of a hairpin bend. At 50 metres per second that climb is over 100 metres. So a mud line on a facing cliff is not a depth. It's a speedometer.

At this border the two arms of the river meet almost exactly head-on, at 96 degrees. Three elevations read off the satellite terrain — the river bed at 1,815 m, a sheltered mud line at 1,875 m, and the facing cliff at 1,920–1,930 m — separate the two effects and give both numbers at once:

What the cliff says The flow was about 60 metres deep and its water was moving at about 30 metres per second. A wave's front travels faster than the water in it — by the speed of a ripple, which in 60 metres of water is 24 m/s — so the front should have been advancing at about 54 m/s. The seismometer and the camera, between them, say 52. Nothing in that chain used the clock, and it lands within a few per cent of it.

That also settled an argument we'd been having with ourselves. The water moves at 30 m/s; the front moves at 54. Those are different things, and we had spent a day confusing them. Independently, geopera worked out from the size of boulders the flood could carry that the water was moving at 19–26 m/s. Two completely unrelated methods, both saying the water was far slower than the wave.

How big was the collapse, really?

This is the number everyone disagrees about, and the disagreement is enormous: published estimates run from half a million cubic metres to two hundred million — a factor of four hundred.

Arguing about it one estimate at a time has got nowhere, so we asked a different question. Instead of "which number is right", we asked which combinations of size, wetness and slipperiness are consistent with everything we can actually measure at once — the seven-minute clock, the speed at the border, the amount of valley floor the flood scoured out, and the amount of debris it left behind. Then we ran the model hundreds of times across the whole disputed range and kept only the versions that passed every test simultaneously.

Most fail. The ones that survive all put the collapse at roughly 14 to 34 million cubic metres — an order of magnitude below the largest published figures, and far narrower than the range we started from. That fits the mountain's own record: when the other side of this same peak collapsed in 2015 and buried Langtang village, the deposit was measured at 6.8 million cubic metres, not hundreds.

We checked whether that answer was really coming from the data or from one of our own assumptions. The debris-volume limit we imposed was the shakiest input, so we changed it by a factor of two and a half and ran the whole thing again. The size answer moved by about a third and the two ranges overlap — so it is the measurements driving it, not the assumption.

What this does not tell us Size is the only thing the survivors agree on. How wet the mass was, how slippery, and how much of it was ice rather than rock are not pinned down — those spanned nearly the entire range we allowed. An earlier version of this analysis appeared to settle them too, and that turned out to be an artefact of the debris limit we later found was too strict. So: we can say how big, with reasonable confidence. We cannot yet say what it was made of, except that the seven-minute clock and the ice visible in the footage both point the same way.

We proved our own model wrong

This is the part we are most pleased with, which sounds odd until you see why.

The model could always drop sediment but never pick any up — a flood that scours a valley floor was, in our equations, incapable of scouring anything. So we built that in, using erosion formulas published for entirely different rivers on other continents, with nothing tuned to this event. It worked better than it had any right to: the model tore 3.8 million cubic metres out of the corridor, against 3.2 million measured from satellite stereo imagery, and it put the erosion in the same reaches.

But the same change broke something else. Now that the model could drop sediment properly, it dropped far too much, and the flood died before it reached the towns downstream. So we tested it honestly: hundreds of runs across every plausible combination of inputs, asking whether any version could arrive on time and leave the right amount of debris. None could. Not one in a hundred and fifty.

That is a real result, and a harder one than a good fit. It doesn't say our numbers were slightly off; it says the shape of the model was wrong. And the fix was to stop pretending "sediment" is one thing:

Two kinds of solid Coarse — boulders and gravel. Heavy, settles out, and carries the grinding friction. This is the rocky core that stops in the gorge and builds the debris fan.

Fine — silt, and ice. Carried along with the water, behaving as part of the fluid rather than as a pile of stones. Silt stays in suspension for hundreds of kilometres; ice simply melts and disappears. Neither leaves the kind of deposit a satellite can measure.

One release can then be dense enough to run fast and clean enough not to bury the valley. With that single change, the previously impossible combination becomes possible.

And here is the part that made us trust it: geopera reached the same conclusion the same week, by a completely different route. They fitted their own model to their satellite measurements using machine-learning optimisation, and it pulled their sediment settling rate down by a factor of three, telling them the material "behaves finer than assumed, remaining in suspension longer". Two groups, two methods, one answer.

A river is a kind of electrical circuit

One more idea from the technical work, because it explains something survivors reported: the flood came in surges, not one clean wave.

A river valley behaves surprisingly like an electrical circuit. Narrow gorges are like wires — the flood shoots through. Wide basins and side valleys are like capacitors — they fill up while the surge passes, then drain back afterwards, smoothing the peak and stretching the tail. Temporary debris dams are switches that hold, then break.

There is a striking example right at the border. The side valley the flood slammed into doesn't just deflect it — the water piles up and runs backwards, uphill, into the valley opposite. Tracing the height of that pile-up on the map, it should reach about 3.5 km up the wrong way. Chinese state media reported that about 3 km of the highway approaching the border port was destroyed. Satellite images show the channel there widened, then tapering back to nothing over the same distance — unlike the two directions the flood genuinely flowed through, which stay wide for kilometres. Three different clues, one answer: a large volume of this flood briefly went the wrong way, and came back late.

We tested the model on other disasters — and withdrew one of the tests

Any model can be tuned to "explain" the event it was built on. The test is whether it works somewhere else. So we ran ours, unchanged, against Chamoli 2021 in India — a similar mountain collapse in opposite conditions: winter, a nearly dry river, and a mass that was four-fifths rock. It failed at first, in a useful way, and chasing the failure taught us the missing physics: how wet a moving mass is controls how much it grinds against itself, and heat takes minutes to soak into ice before it melts. With those pieces added, one set of equations explains both events. That test stands.

A second test, against the 2012 Seti River flood in Nepal, we reported as a clean pass. We have withdrawn it. The river channel that test ran on was built by a routine that fills gaps in the map data with straight lines — and it had flown those straight lines across a mountain rim. The repair meant to clean up bad elevations then flattened 31 of the 54 kilometres to zero slope. The model had been slowed by terrain that did not exist. On a channel with a real gradient, the same model arrives four times too early, and a second conclusion we drew from that test reverses outright. Both are off the table.

Why we're telling you this A withdrawn result is not a footnote. We found it ourselves, while building something unrelated, and it cost us one of three headline findings. The alternative — leaving it standing because it was flattering — is how a project like this becomes worthless. Everything here is dated, and anything we retract stays visible with the reason.

The seven-minute problem — and the seven-hour opportunity

The hardest fact in this event is that the people closest to the mountain could not have been saved by any warning system that has to involve a human being. Seven minutes is not enough to notice, decide, call, and have someone run. For the border crossing, only a machine deciding on its own — a seismometer wired directly to a siren — would have worked.

But that is not true of the rest of the river. Betrawati had 43 minutes. Galchhi had over two hours. Devghat had seven. Those are ordinary warning timescales, comfortably enough for phone trees, sirens and evacuation — and most of the people who died were in that stretch, not the first 22 kilometres.

The seismic network detected the collapse within minutes. It logged it as an earthquake. The instrument worked; the interpretation didn't, and the warning went out 38 minutes later. That is a software and institutional problem, not a sensor problem, and it is much cheaper to fix than new monitoring hardware.

An independent audit by geopera of whether this collapse could have been predicted came back null on all four satellite channels: under two metres of creep in a hundred days, steady, with no acceleration in the final 48 hours; ordinary glacier thinning; and a warm summer nearly identical to the two before it that produced no collapse. You cannot watch every peak. But you do not have to predict the collapse to save the people two hours downstream — you only have to notice it and route it. Their phrase for it is exact: "Those hours existed physically. They were lost institutionally."

How this was made

This analysis was done by one person in the evenings, working with an AI assistant, over ten days. That is unusual enough that it should be on the page rather than in a footnote — partly so you can judge it, and partly because the division of labour turned out to be the interesting part.

The method, plainly

Who and what. Dave Hume, an engineer in New Zealand, working with Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic) running in Claude Code — a terminal tool where the model can write and run programs, read and edit files, search the web and publish pages, rather than just answer questions. Every model run, every satellite download, every chart on this page was produced that way.

How the work actually split. The AI wrote the physics code, ran the simulations, fetched and processed the satellite imagery, did the literature searches and drafted the prose. The human set the direction, brought the original hypothesis, and — this is the part that matters — did most of the catching.

Five of the errors corrected this week were found because the human pushed back, not because the model checked itself:

  • "I don't follow the briefly-impounded logic" — it was wrong; damming that river for the volume claimed would have taken 37 hours, not minutes.
  • "Is there an energy mismatch?" — there wasn't, but chasing it found a real soft spot in how the model ties speed to size.
  • "Won't that depend on the angle of the side valleys?" — it does, and the run-up formula had been stated too loosely.
  • "What do they say about the 10-minute difference?" — they said nothing, and going to find out uncovered the timestamp that saved the finding.
  • "Rerun it with the relaxed limit" — which exposed three numbers we had been quoting as artefacts of a constraint that was too strict.

The human also made two measurements the model could not: reading the mud lines off the satellite terrain, and spotting from a flood animation that another group's model was missing the flow that went backwards up the Chinese valley.

What the AI was good for. Speed of checking. A hypothesis could be tested in minutes rather than an evening, which meant bad ideas were killed quickly instead of being defended. The satellite work — finding the scenes, compositing five cloudy passes into one usable image, measuring channel widening — took an hour rather than a week. And it never minded being told it was wrong, which is the trait this particular job most needed.

What it was bad at. Confident errors in its own fresh work. Two bugs on the last day were entirely self-inflicted: sediment being counted twice in the ledger, and a chart whose bars were drawn to the wrong axis. Both were in things written minutes earlier and believed. Neither would have been caught by asking the model whether it was sure.

Rules we worked under. The project has written rules, kept in the repository since day one: state conclusions as ranges over disputed inputs rather than single numbers; never tune a parameter to fit the very observation you are scoring against, and if you must, declare it and hold other measurements back as a check; report failures with their diagnostic shape rather than hiding them; credit the researchers who got there first — the contribution here is arithmetic, not correction. Where those rules were broken, it is noted above.

Checkable. Every number here comes from code in a public repository that runs on open data in seconds. The full development history, including every retraction and the reasoning behind it, is in the commit log. Nothing rests on trusting either of us.

What we're not sure of

The honesty box

Retracted or reversed this week, in order: the Seti River test, entirely; the idea that a briefly dammed river could have supplied the flood's volume (blocking that river for 37 hours would be needed, and it was minutes); reading the 70-metre mud lines as flow depth; then over-correcting and reading them as pure run-up; and a rule-of-thumb for wave volume that assumed the wave travelled as a rigid block, which our own earlier work had already disproved.

The size of the collapse is genuinely unknown. Published estimates span a factor of four hundred. Our envelope is much narrower, but it comes from requiring consistency across several measurements rather than from measuring the mountain, and it depends on a debris-volume figure that is itself a lower bound taken from less than half the valley floor.

Our model puts the debris in the wrong place. Satellite stereo shows the main deposit 40–43 km downstream, where the valley opens. Ours drops it too early. geopera's model got this right and ours did not.

One number in the two-solids fix is fitted, not derived — the split between coarse and fine. We say so rather than bury it, and we kept two downstream measurements out of the fitting so they could act as an honest check. They improved sharply, which is the evidence that the fix is physics rather than curve-fitting.

We ran one last test and it half failed. Two downstream measurements were deliberately kept out of the fitting so they could act as an honest check. The flood volume arriving at Devghat came out right for every surviving version — the best of them within a per cent of the observed figure. But the height the river rose at Galchhi came out at about 3.6 metres against roughly 9 observed. The model routes the right amount of water and then converts it into the wrong height, which points at our estimate of the river's shape in its lower reaches rather than at the flood physics. Below the halfway point we have no measured cross-sections, only a rule of thumb. We are publishing that failure rather than quietly dropping the test.

This is fast, independent, AI-assisted analysis published days after a disaster. It has not been peer-reviewed. Where our numbers disagree with an agency's, both are shown. Where other groups got somewhere first or got it better, we say so — geopera's satellite work is better than ours, and their sediment result arrived independently and confirms ours.

What would help most

Three things, and none of them require new instruments:

The full gauge records from Galchhi and Devghat, held by Nepal's hydrology department. A photograph has no time axis, so no amount of satellite imagery can measure how much water passed. Only a gauge trace can, and it would settle the volume argument everyone is having.

Hydropower plant records — headworks sensors, gate logs, control-system archives across the thirteen affected projects, including plants that were damaged but whose data survived off-site. These plausibly hold the only high-frequency measurements of the flood's upper reach, where the public gauges were destroyed.

Repeat satellite elevation surveys over the next few weeks. If the deposit was ice-rich, it must go on losing volume as buried ice melts out. Rock doesn't do that. Two surveys a month apart would separate the two directly, with no model involved.

The one-paragraph version

In short A mountainside fell into a monsoon-swollen river. It could not have melted enough ice to make the flood — the physics of melting forbids it — so the water was the river's own, swept up and delivered at once. The collapse reached a border camera 22 km away in seven minutes, which only something running like slush can do; ice is visible in the footage. Mud lines on a cliff where the flow hit head-on give the wave's depth and speed independently, and they agree with the clock. The collapse was probably far smaller and far wetter than the largest published estimates. Our model, given the ability to scour a valley, matched the measured erosion — and then failed a test we designed to break it, which told us the flood was carrying two different kinds of sediment, not one. For the first 22 km no human warning chain could have worked. For the next 150 there were hours, and they were lost to a system that logged a mountain collapse as an earthquake.
