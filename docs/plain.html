The Water Was Already in the River

On 26 August 2026 a piece of a Himalayan mountain fell into a river valley in Rasuwa, Nepal. Seven minutes and forty seconds later a wall of ice, rock and mud taller than a ten-storey building destroyed the border crossing at Rasuwagadhi, 22 kilometres away, and crossed into China. More than 1,340 people are dead and nearly 4,900 missing. This is a plain-English account of where all that water came from, how we tested the idea, what we got wrong along the way, and what it means for keeping people alive next time. The full technical version, with every number and every assumption on display, is here.

What happened

Langtang Lirung is a 7,200-metre peak about 60 kilometres north of Kathmandu. Its north face stands above a small mountain stream, the Chhochen Khola, which feeds a small river, the Lhende Khola. At 8:37 on the morning of 26 August 2026 — clear, no rain, late monsoon — a piece of that face about 600 metres wide let go from around 5,200 metres up. The mountain shook hard enough to register as an earthquake of magnitude 5. A drone at a Chinese radar post happened to be filming: a white stream of ice leading a brown cloud of rock dust. Every scientist who has looked at the pictures says the same thing: the bedrock failed and took a hanging glacier down with it. Nobody has said how much of it was ice, and estimates of how much fell in total differ by a factor of four hundred.

The mass dropped about 1,200 metres onto the moraine and loose ground below the face, scouring it as it went, then another thousand metres to the stream bed, and turned into a torrent of ice, rock and mud that ran 22 kilometres down the Lhende — a gorge whose river, for the last 14 of those kilometres, is the border between Nepal and China. It covered that distance in seven minutes and forty seconds, an average of about 172 km/h, and it did not slow down on the way. (This page said "seven minutes" and 190 km/h until 6 September; the correction is further down.) It left a dam of debris in the upper valley that held back a lake for two days before draining, and it scoured the gorge floor down by several metres rather than filling it.

At 8:44:50 it arrived at the place where the Lhende runs almost head-on into a much bigger river, the Kyirong Tsangpo, which becomes Nepal's Bhote Koshi. The Chinese border post, Gyirong Port, stood on the far bank directly opposite; Nepal's Rasuwagadhi customs and a 111-megawatt hydropower plant stood on the near side. The port's security camera filmed what arrived, and a Chinese description of the footage calls it "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks". We measured the front off that footage at about 170 km/h as it went past. A six-storey building on the port's terrace was not damaged; it was removed. Mud lines and stripped vegetation on the valley walls show the flow was about 60 metres deep here and climbed 45–55 metres higher still up the cliff it struck, which — as a later section explains — is how fast it was moving, written on the rock. Some of it was thrown backwards up the Chinese arm of the valley: about 3 km of the highway approaching the port was destroyed.

From there it was a flood, and a river's worth of water joined it. The dry port at Timure and the Friendship Bridge, both rebuilt after a glacial flood down the same valley in July 2025, went again. At Syabrubesi, 38 kilometres from the mountain, the river gauge was reading 3.8 metres and rising at 8:50 when it stopped transmitting; the town was under water by about 9:15, and here, where the valley opens out, the flood slowed sharply and dumped its heaviest load — a sheet of boulders and gravel three kilometres long and up to 18 metres thick, with individual rocks the size of houses. Betrawati's gauge went at 9:20. Four river gauges were destroyed in all. Below Betrawati the river is the Trishuli, one of the most densely developed hydropower corridors in the Himalaya, and its tunnels and camps were full of workers on a Wednesday morning: several hundred were brought out alive over the following days, hundreds more were not.

The gauge at Galchhi, 108 kilometres down, survived and recorded the river rising nine metres in half an hour around 11:00. Malekhu lost its bridge at 11:43. The peak passed Muglin in the early afternoon and reached Devghat, where the Trishuli meets the Kali Gandaki, at four in the afternoon — seven hours after the collapse. Bodies were recovered as far as 240 kilometres away, across the border in India. The first SMS warnings went out at 9:15, 38 minutes after the collapse. For everyone in the upper valley the flood had already come and gone. Thirteen power stations were damaged. As of 5 September the reported toll stood at 1,342 dead and 4,886 missing in Nepal, with 31 dead and 531 missing on the Chinese side, and more than 5,300 injured. The missing count has started to fall while the deaths rise — that is bodies being recovered rather than people found alive, and of roughly 900 recovered by 1 September only about 4% had been identified. Every figure here should be read as provisional.

By four in the afternoon, at Devghat — 199 river-kilometres away by the channel we traced, 168 on the shorter reckoning the official report uses — the river crested at roughly four times its normal monsoon flow. Nepal's Flood Forecasting Division estimated the event pushed about 20 million cubic metres of "extra" water down the river — around 8,000 Olympic swimming pools — in a single afternoon.

What is known, and what is not

Known, and measured by someone: when it started, to the second; when it reached the border, to the second; the river heights at five gauges before four of them were destroyed; the peak at Devghat; how much of the gorge floor was scoured and where the main deposit lies; the height of the mud lines at the border.

Not known by anyone yet: how much fell, and how much of it was ice; whether the avalanche gouged the glacier it landed on; what the flow looked like, minute by minute, at the gauges that were destroyed; how far it went up the Chinese valley; where along the river the dead were. Everything on this page that depends on one of those is stated as a range, and the technical report lists what would settle each.

The puzzle

A falling mountain is rock and ice. Rock doesn't flood. So where did 8,000 pools of water come from? The standard first guess after disasters like this is melted glacier ice — the fall grinds the ice up, friction heats it, and it melts into flood water.

We doubted that, for a reason you can check with high-school physics.

Falling a kilometre doesn't buy much melting

When something falls, its energy of height turns into heat. A fall of 1,200 metres — roughly what this mass fell before it hit the river — gives each kilogram of material only enough heat to melt about 35 grams of ice, and that's if every scrap of the heat goes into melting, which it never does.

How much water that adds up to depends on how much fell, and that is the number nobody agrees on. So we did it twice. At the collapse size our own evidence supports — 14 to 34 million cubic metres, which is a later section of this page — melting supplies about 1 million cubic metres of water, or 2.5 million if you turn every dial in melting's favour at once: the top of that size range, four-fifths ice, the longest plausible drop, and the most generous heat-sharing anyone has published. Against 20 million of "extra" water, that is short by a factor of eight.

Melting only reaches the official figure if you take the very largest published collapse — 200 million cubic metres, six times the top of our own range — and also assume four-fifths ice, a 4,000-metre drop and the most generous heat-sharing. Do all four at once and you get 20.7 million: it arrives exactly, and only, at the end of a chain where every link is set to its most favourable value. Relax any one of them and it falls away fast.

So most of the flood's water was not made by the mountain. It was already in the valley.

The thing that surprises everyone Warming ice is nearly free. Melting it is enormously expensive. Raising a kilogram of ice by one degree takes about 2,100 joules; actually melting that kilogram takes 334,000 — you could warm ice by 159 degrees for the price of melting it once. So it makes almost no difference whether the glacier started at −20 °C or −0.1 °C: the two differ by about 12% in what it costs to turn them into water. That is why this argument is robust even though nobody has ever measured that glacier's temperature. And pressure doesn't rescue it either — squeezing ice does lower its melting point, but only by about 0.0074 °C per atmosphere, and the effect is reversible. Release the pressure and it freezes straight back. Pressure moves the phase boundary; it doesn't pay the bill.

The snowplow

Picture the monsoon-swollen river as a long line of water lying in the valley, already flowing. Now send a fast, heavy wave down it. The wave acts like a snowplow: its front overruns the slower river water ahead of it, sweeps it up, and pushes it along. Kilometre after kilometre, the wave grows by collecting river that was already there.

When we do the arithmetic along the whole 199 km, roughly three-quarters of the water that passed the downstream gauge was ordinary river water, gathered up and delivered all at once. The mountain provided the push; the monsoon provided the water. Other Himalayan and Andean disasters, when examined closely, have told the same story.

This distinction is not academic. If flood volume comes mostly from the valley's own river, then the size of a future flood depends on the season — the same collapse in February and in August produce utterly different disasters. Warning systems should know that.

It did not fall like dry rock

Remember the border camera: 22 km in seven minutes forty. We simulated the collapse many different ways — small and large, dry rock, icy, wet — and asked which versions could reach that camera on time. The result was stark. Every dry-rock version arrived late — 17 to 30 minutes, never under eight — no matter how big we made it. Every icy or wet version arrived within a few minutes of it — inside the tolerance the test allows; the chart below shows how close each came.

The physics is intuitive once said aloud: dry broken rock fights itself. Every fragment grinds against its neighbours, burning off the energy of the fall — like a truckload of gravel dumped down a slope, violent but self-braking. Ice and water lubricate. A wet or icy mass keeps the fall's energy as speed. Only something lubricated — by ice, by water, or both — covers that gorge in under eight minutes. (Until 6 September this sentence said "only something running like slush", which claimed more than the test shows; the next section says what the lubricant most likely was.)

Be careful which way that arrow points. The test excludes dry rock; it does not establish slush. "Not dry" is a wide category, and our own size-and-composition search later on this page cannot narrow it: how wet the mass was, and how much of it was ice rather than rock, come out spanning almost the entire range we allowed. So the honest claim is the negative one, and the heading above says so. What we can add to it is circumstantial and consistent — ice is visible in the border footage, and several groups describe a bedrock failure that carried a hanging glacier with it.

Correction, 6 September This page previously said that where two ice grains touch, the contact stress of order 10,000 atmospheres collapses the melting point by about 70 °C. That was a straight-line extrapolation of the 0.0074 °C-per-atmosphere figure in the callout above, and it does not hold that far — which the callout itself should have warned us about, since it says in as many words that pressure "doesn't pay the bill". Ordinary ice ceases to exist at about 2,000 atmospheres, where the melting point has fallen roughly 22 °C; past that, the high-pressure forms of ice melt at higher temperatures, not lower. So pressure melting is a small effect with a hard floor, and the number was wrong in size and, beyond 2,000 atmospheres, in direction. The conclusion it was offered in support of is unaffected and arguably strengthened: the film that lubricates ice is there anyway, without needing to be squeezed into existence.

Ice and rock together are not rock — think of an ice skate

Added 6 September, evening, after Dave Hume asked whether the first 22 kilometres had much water in them at all.

Start with how much water there was to sweep up before the border. The Lhende is a small river: about 150 cubic metres a second where it meets the big one, far less higher up. All the water lying in its 22 kilometres of channel that morning adds up to about half a million cubic metres. Add the water squeezed out of the saturated gravel the torrent tore up, and the little that friction had melted by then, and you get perhaps two to four million. The mountain that came down that valley was fourteen to thirty-four million. So what hit the border post was at least four-fifths mountain: rock and ice, with some mud. "A black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks" is a good description of a debris flow, and the mud lines at the border — sixty metres deep, moving at thirty metres a second — are describing a mass of rock and ice hitting a wall, not a wave of water.

The water story starts at the junction. The big river coming out of Tibet carried about 150 cubic metres a second, and the 177 kilometres of river below held some 22 million cubic metres of water at that moment. The snowplow described above is what happened from the border onward. Nothing in the melting arithmetic changes; what changes is the map. Above the junction there was almost no river to sweep. Below it there was a great deal.

That leaves the question of how fourteen to thirty-four million cubic metres of rock and ice got 22 kilometres down a gorge in seven minutes forty. The answer is the ice — not as water but as ice, and the physics is the physics of an ice skate.

Why ice is slippery

It is not, mostly, because pressure melts it. Squeezing ice does lower its melting point, but only by 0.0074 °C per atmosphere, and you can skate at −30 °C where no skate blade could ever press hard enough. The first reason is premelting: an ice surface carries a disordered, liquid-like film even far below freezing, with no load on it at all. Michael Faraday noticed it in the 1850s. The film thickens as the temperature climbs toward zero, and it is why ice is slippery when nothing is moving.

The second reason is the one that runs an avalanche: friction makes its own meltwater. Rub ice and the heat goes into the surface. At skating speeds that is a film a fraction of a hair thick. Under thirty metres of rock and ice sliding at forty metres a second, the heat released at the sliding surface is around two megawatts per square metre — enough to melt about seven millimetres of ice off the base every second, whatever the temperature of the ice above it. The lubricating film renews itself faster than it can be squeezed out, from the moment the mass is moving fast. The energy this takes over the whole run is a few per cent of the energy of the fall, so it makes almost no flood water: it is lubrication, not production, and the melting limit earlier on this page is untouched. It also feeds on itself. Faster sliding makes more melt, which makes the surface slipperier, which makes it faster still, until the base is fully lubricated and the speed levels off. A mass running on that kind of friction accelerates to a plateau and holds it. Ours did: our measurement at the border, 47 metres a second, is the same as the average over the whole 22 kilometres, 47.8. It did not slow down in the gorge. Dry rock, grinding on itself, would have slowed the whole way.

The third reason is that ice melting inside the moving mass turns a pile of grinding stones into a slurry that floats its own weight on water. That is what our model's "slipperiness dial" stands for, and in the model, ice counts on the fluid side of the dial. So when the collapse-size search on this page says the runs that pass are "wet or icy", read it as: enough ice or water to lubricate. It does not mean the mass was mostly water. It almost certainly was not.

It has happened before

In September 2002 the Kolka glacier in the Russian Caucasus let go — something like a hundred million cubic metres, most of it glacier ice — and ran about 19 kilometres down the Genaldon valley at 50 to 80 metres a second, which is four to six minutes, before the debris jammed at a narrow gorge and the watery part of it ran on another 15 kilometres as a mudflow. About 125 to 140 people died. The Lhende run is that kind of event at a fifth of the size: a similar distance in a similar time, a body of debris that stopped where the valley made it stop, and a flood that carried on. The contrast is Chamoli in India in 2021, which was four-fifths rock: it took 34 to 37 minutes to cover a similar distance, stopping and starting, and it is the event our model was tested against in a later section.

What this changes on this page is mostly words. Where we said "wall of water" for what hit the border, we now say ice, rock and mud; the flood is what it became. It does not by itself change the size of the collapse we estimate, which rests on the clock, the scour and the deposit, none of which cares how the mass got slippery — though two later measurements, described under "How big was the collapse", say that estimate is probably too small. It does not tell us how much of it was ice. And it makes the warning problem no easier: a face of rock and ice above a monsoon river produces a front at 170 kilometres an hour whether or not the river below is high.

The seven minutes nearly came apart

That whole finding rests on one number, the border clock. So when another analysis — geopera, doing careful satellite work and publishing openly — put the border arrival at seventeen minutes, it was serious. Say the uncomfortable version out loud: our dry-rock simulations arrive at seventeen minutes exactly. If seventeen is right, then the simplest reading of the whole event is a dry rock avalanche, and our finding is not just wrong, it is backwards.

Chasing it down took a day. They never mention the camera at all: their method is openly licensed satellite data, and the camera footage had been actively censored — two clips of the border gate going down were blocked on Chinese platforms, even inside private one-to-one messages (China Digital Times, 30 August). It wasn't available to them. Worse, the fact-checkers who did see the footage converted its on-screen Beijing time to Nepal time and reported it loosely as "around 9 o'clock in Nepal" — which, read literally, would be about twenty-three minutes. That is a second independent vote for the slow answer, and it is the version we had to break.

The resolution was sitting in Chinese-language reporting: the footage is stamped 10:59 Beijing time, which is 8:44 in Nepal — the conversion had already been done correctly at the source, and the fact-checkers' "around 9 o'clock" is a loose paraphrase of 8:44 rather than a different reading.

Then we stopped taking anyone's word for it — 6 September

Everything above rests on reports of what the camera says, and the report carrying the overlay verbatim is a partisan outlet, the Epoch Times. So we obtained the footage and read the timestamp directly. The station's own clock is legible in the video, and it advances in step with real time — checked at four separate points, with no sign of the clip having been cut or re-timed. The partisan source is now corroboration rather than the thing holding the argument up.

And it corrected our own figure. The destruction begins at 10:59:50 Beijing — 08:44:50 in Nepal — which is 7 minutes 40 seconds after the collapse, not the 6 minutes 50 seconds this page carried until today. That older number is exactly what you get by reading the overlay to the minute and assuming the seconds were zero. They are about fifty. So the average speed over the first 22 kilometres is 47.8 metres per second, not 53.7 — about 12% slower.

The finding stands. Seventeen minutes is still excluded, and so is twenty-three. Late on 6 September the model runs were repeated at the corrected time — a check that turned up the fact that the correction had reached every page and none of the code — and the size range barely moved (13 to 34 million cubic metres, median 23, against 14 to 34 and median 21 before). But it was our number and it was wrong, so it is corrected here rather than quietly adjusted.

Kargel's matching figure is probably not independent. The glaciologist Jeffrey Kargel published 6 minutes 50 seconds and we originally presented that as independent confirmation. It now looks like the same minute-rounding arriving by a different road: his stated basis is "22 km at 193 km/h", he does not say where the speed came from, and 6:50 is what the rounding produces. Two accounts agreeing because they made the same simplification is not two pieces of evidence.

What actually carries the argument is neither of those. It is a Nepali gauge at Syabrubesi, 37.6 kilometres down the river, running on Nepali time and owing nothing to any camera, timezone or outlet. It read 3.8 metres and rising at 08:50 — thirteen minutes after the collapse. You cannot take seventeen minutes to reach kilometre 22 and thirteen minutes to reach kilometre 37.6. That single instrument is why the border clock stands.

The same Chinese description of the footage gave us something we had been hunting for days. The wave that hit the border was "a black wall of water carrying ice, mud and massive rocks". Ice, visible, 22 kilometres from the mountain — at exactly the place where the timing argument is made.

How we clocked the front off the video

Added 6 September. This is the measurement the home page lists under "not yet promoted" — a day old at the time of writing, and described here so that it can be checked rather than trusted.

Reading the camera's clock gave the arrival time. Getting a speed out of the same footage needed three more things: the true frame rate, a way to turn pixels into metres, and two marks on the ground a known distance apart.

Frames. The clip is 60 frames a second, but internet video often repeats frames, and a standard tool told us only 24 of every 60 were real. It was wrong — it drops frames that merely look alike, and on grainy security footage that is most of them. A strict test that compares every pixel found 48 to 57 real frames a second, 57 during the arrival. One frame is a sixtieth of a second. An earlier version of this analysis had resampled the video to 30 frames a second and thrown half the information away without noticing.

Metres. The border post's facade is symmetrical about the national emblem, and the row of square dentils under its roofline is, in effect, a ruler. Dave counted 18 of them from the centre to the end of the building, over a half-length of 33.5 metres, which makes each one a 1.97-metre module — a two-metre architectural grid. Separately, he located the camera and both ends of the building on Google Earth and measured the bearings and distances: 117 metres to the left end, 145 to the right. The two methods give the same building length within 2.5% and the same camera-to-building distance within 1.6%. That agreement is what makes the rest trustworthy. It also shows that a metre near the left of the picture is 45% more pixels than a metre near the right, so no single "pixels per metre" number is valid across the frame.

Marks. The debris front crossed the first line of sight and, 1.066 seconds later, the second. Fifty metres in 1.066 seconds is 46.9 metres a second, 169 km/h. The second crossing is hard to pick to better than a frame — the front blurs into a shadow on the cliff behind — and we deliberately took the later, slower frame rather than guessing between them. That choice is worth about 2 m/s. The distance is worth far more: the shortest defensible reading of the gap is 45 metres and the longest 65, so the honest range is 41 to 63 metres a second, 150 to 223 km/h, with 47 the best estimate rather than the middle. One question is still open: if the 50 metres was read at the building rather than where the debris actually passed, further from the camera, the gap is nearer 69 metres and the speed nearer 68 m/s, because the two lines spread apart with distance.

What it agrees with. The clock gives 47.8 m/s averaged over the 22 kilometres. geopera's mud-line method gives 45–52 at the same place. Our three mud lines, read as depth plus run-up, give 54. All three sit inside 41 to 63. The one published figure that does not is the 19 m/s from the frame-by-frame study by the Chinese Academy of Sciences — and that, we think, is because it measured a different thing: the surface of the water after it had turned the corner, not the front of the debris as it arrived. A front at 19 would need to climb the cliff with more energy than it had, which is impossible; a front at 47 climbs it with about half its energy, which is normal. Three unrelated methods landing within 2 m/s of each other is partly luck, and we say so: the honest statement is the range.

What did not work, so that nobody repeats it: tracking the dark plume sideways across the picture (it billows toward the camera rather than moving across it, and a line fitted to it returned nonsense); tracking the edge of the pale plume (it gave anything between 12 and 58 m/s depending on settings, which is not a measurement); and a geometric reconstruction with the river almost in line with the camera, which turned every pixel of error into metres. Three claims made during that day's work were withdrawn the same day and are listed in the project dossier.

Reading a flood off a cliff

Floods leave mud lines. The trick is knowing what a given line means, because a line's height depends on where it is.

On a sheltered wall, the flow slides past and the mud line sits near the real water surface. But where a flow drives head-on into a cliff, it stops dead and converts all its speed into height — the same way water climbs the far bank of a hairpin bend. At 50 metres per second that climb is over 100 metres. So a mud line on a facing cliff is not a depth. It's a speedometer.

At this border the flood's valley runs into the main river valley at 96 degrees — very nearly a right angle, which means the flow cannot turn the corner and instead strikes the cliff on the far side of the junction almost square-on. That is the head-on case, and it is why this particular junction can be read as a speedometer. Three elevations read off the satellite terrain — the river bed at 1,815 m, a sheltered mud line at 1,875 m, and the facing cliff at 1,920–1,930 m — separate the two effects and give both numbers at once:

What the cliff says The flow was about 60 metres deep and its water was moving at about 30 metres per second. A wave's front travels faster than the water in it — by the speed of a ripple, which in 60 metres of water is 24 m/s — so the front should have been advancing at about 54 m/s. The seismometer and the camera, between them, say 47.8 on average over the 22 kilometres, and our own reading of the video at this spot (next section) says 47. Nothing in that chain used the clock, and it lands within about 15% of it — close, but not the near-exact match this box claimed while the clock still read 52.

That also settled an argument we'd been having with ourselves. The water moves at 30 m/s; the front moves at 54. Those are different things, and we had spent a day confusing them. Independently, geopera worked out from the size of boulders the flood could carry that the water was moving at 19–26 m/s. Two unrelated methods, both saying the water was far slower than the wave — though note that our 30 sits just above their range rather than inside it.

And the speed at this junction is the number we reproduce worst of anything on this page. There are now five estimates of it and they do not agree: the mud lines say the front was doing 54 m/s, geopera's superelevation work says 45–52, our own video measurement says 47 (envelope 41–63), our routing model fits 31, and the first peer-reviewed study of the event — a frame-by-frame analysis of the video by the Chinese Academy of Sciences, published 1 September — measures the surface moving at just 19 m/s. Some of that spread is real physics, because a descent speed, a splash-line speed and a post-turn speed are three different quantities at a junction like this one. Some of it is us being wrong. Late on 6 September we re-ran the size test against the 19, and the answer was sharper than a moved range: nothing passes, at any size, because no run can reach the border on time and be moving at 19 metres a second when it gets there. That is what you would expect if the 19 is the water after the turn rather than the front, which is our reading; it is also what you would see if our own speed-to-arrival physics were wrong. Both scorings are published.

How big was the collapse, really?

This is the number everyone disagrees about, and the disagreement is enormous: published estimates run from half a million cubic metres to two hundred million — a factor of four hundred.

Arguing about it one estimate at a time has got nowhere, so we asked a different question. Instead of "which number is right", we asked which combinations of size, wetness and slipperiness are consistent with everything we can actually measure at once — the border clock, the speed at the border, the amount of valley floor the flood scoured out, and the amount of debris it left behind. Then we ran the model hundreds of times across the whole disputed range and kept only the versions that passed every test simultaneously.

Most fail. The ones that pass all put the collapse at roughly 14 to 34 million cubic metres — an order of magnitude below the largest published figures, and far narrower than the range we started from. That fits the mountain's own record: when the other side of this same peak collapsed in 2015 and buried Langtang village, the deposit was measured at 6.8 million cubic metres, not hundreds.

We checked whether that answer was really coming from the data or from one of our own assumptions. The debris-volume limit we imposed was the shakiest input, so we changed it by a factor of two and a half and ran the whole thing again. The size answer moved by about a third and the two ranges overlap — so it is the measurements driving it, not the assumption.

Late on 6 September: this number is probably too small

Two measurements made after the rest of this page was written say the collapse was bigger than 14 to 34 million cubic metres. Neither uses anything the estimate above was built on.

The Chinese arm. Part of the flow ran about three kilometres up the main valley on the Chinese side and came back. From the shape of that valley and the satellite images, the arm held somewhere between 13 and 70 million cubic metres at the peak. Divide that by the share of the flow that went up the arm — anywhere from a tenth to a half; the geometry of the junction cannot narrow it — and the volume that passed the border comes out inside our range at one corner and two to twenty times above it everywhere else.

A mud line 21 kilometres downstream. Helicopter footage of the Upper Trishuli-1 headworks shows the flood's mud line roughly 45 to 70 metres above the old river bed, read four ways: a tunnel portal as a ruler, the benches of debris below it, geopera's measurement that the bed rose 11 to 18 metres there, and the road ledge on the map. Our model runs that pass every other test put the water 5 to 21 metres deep at that spot.

The estimate above was scored on timing, a speed, how much the valley was scoured and how much debris was left. It was never scored on how deep the water got. When it is, we expect the number to go up — how far, we cannot yet say — and for the debris limit to force the answer toward a mass that was mostly ice and fine sediment, which is what everything else here points to. Until that is run, read the size above as an estimate under revision.

What this does not tell us Size is the only thing the passing runs agree on. How wet the mass was, how slippery, and how much of it was ice rather than rock are not pinned down — those spanned nearly the entire range we allowed. An earlier version of this analysis appeared to settle them too, and that turned out to be an artefact of the debris limit we later found was too strict. So: we can say how big, with reasonable confidence. We cannot yet say what it was made of, except that the border clock and the ice visible in the footage both point the same way.

We proved our own model wrong

This is the part of the work we would defend hardest, which sounds odd until you see why.

The model could always drop sediment but never pick any up — a flood that scours a valley floor was, in our equations, incapable of scouring anything. So we built that in, using erosion formulas published for entirely different rivers on other continents, with nothing tuned to this event. It worked better than it had any right to: the model tore 3.8 million cubic metres out of the corridor, against 3.2 million measured from satellite stereo imagery, and it put the erosion in the same reaches.

But the same change broke something else. Now that the model could drop sediment properly, it dropped far too much, and the flood stalled before it reached the towns downstream. So we tested it honestly: hundreds of runs across every plausible combination of inputs, asking whether any version could arrive on time and leave the right amount of debris. None could. Not one in a hundred and fifty.

That is a real result, and a harder one than a good fit. It doesn't say our numbers were slightly off; it says the shape of the model was wrong. And the fix was to stop pretending "sediment" is one thing:

Two kinds of solid Coarse — boulders and gravel. Heavy, settles out, and carries the grinding friction. This is the rocky core that stops in the gorge and builds the debris fan.

Fine — silt, and ice. Carried along with the water, behaving as part of the fluid rather than as a pile of stones. Silt stays in suspension for hundreds of kilometres; ice simply melts and disappears. Neither leaves the kind of deposit a satellite can measure.

One release can then be dense enough to run fast and clean enough not to bury the valley. With that single change, the previously impossible combination becomes possible.

And here is the part that made us trust it. geopera reached the same conclusion the same week by a completely different route: fitting their own model to their satellite measurements with machine-learning optimisation, which pulled their sediment settling rate down by a factor of three and told them the material "behaves finer than assumed, remaining in suspension longer". Two methods, one answer — but be clear about how much weight that carries. geopera is not an institution: it is Geopera Pty Ltd, and the analysis is by its founder, Darcy Weedman, published on a company blog and not peer-reviewed, exactly like this one. Two unreviewed analyses agreeing is real evidence and it is not the same as replication. Their two posts also give different border arrival times, 17 minutes in one and 23 in another, which is worth knowing when we lean on them elsewhere. Their satellite work is better than ours and we lean on it a lot; we would rather name the weight than borrow it.

A river is a kind of electrical circuit

One more idea from the technical work, because it explains something survivors reported: the flood came in surges, not one clean wave.

A river valley behaves surprisingly like an electrical circuit. Narrow gorges are like wires — the flood shoots through. Wide basins and side valleys are like capacitors — they fill up while the surge passes, then drain back afterwards, smoothing the peak and stretching the tail. Temporary debris dams are switches that hold, then break.

There is a striking example right at the border. The flood came down a side valley, the Lhende, and slammed into the main one. The main valley doesn't just deflect it — the water piles up and runs backwards, uphill, into the main valley's upstream arm. Tracing the height of that pile-up on the map, it should reach about 3.5 km up the wrong way. China's own reconstruction says part of the flow ran "nearly 3 km upstream", and Chinese state media reported that about 3 km of the highway approaching the border port was destroyed. Satellite images, measured along the river on 6 September, show the ground stripped bare up that arm fading out between about 2 and 3.5 km from the junction — while the two directions the flood genuinely flowed through stay stripped for the whole 5–8 km we could see. Sharper commercial imagery shows fresh sediment on the valley floor a further kilometre or so beyond that, which may be the surge or may be what settled in the reach that ponded behind the debris plug at the junction; the technical report keeps both readings. Four different clues, one answer: a large volume of this flood briefly went about three kilometres the wrong way, and came back late.

We tested the model on other disasters — and withdrew one of the tests

Any model can be tuned to "explain" the event it was built on. The test is whether it works somewhere else. So we ran ours, unchanged, against Chamoli 2021 in India — a similar mountain collapse in opposite conditions: winter, a nearly dry river, and a mass that was four-fifths rock. It failed at first, in a useful way, and chasing the failure taught us the missing physics: how wet a moving mass is controls how much it grinds against itself, and heat takes minutes to soak into ice before it melts. With those pieces added, one set of equations explains both events. That test stands — but as a partial pass, not a clean one. The model now delivers the front to Tapovan in 36.3 minutes against an observed 34–37, which is the headline number; inside the same run it crosses the Raini basin in 13 minutes against the roughly 27 an independent analysis published for that reach. When we fixed a bug in the arrival calculation, the arrival got better and the basin got worse. Both halves are in the technical report, because reporting only the half that worked is how a stress test stops being one.

A second test, against the 2012 Seti River flood in Nepal, we reported as a clean pass. We have withdrawn it. The river channel that test ran on was built by a routine that fills gaps in the map data with straight lines — and it had flown those straight lines across a mountain rim. The repair meant to clean up bad elevations then flattened 31 of the 54 kilometres to zero slope. The model had been slowed by terrain that did not exist. On a channel with a real gradient, the same model arrives four times too early, and a second conclusion we drew from that test reverses outright. Both are off the table.

Why we're telling you this A withdrawn result is not a footnote. We found it ourselves, while building something unrelated, and it cost us one of three headline findings. The alternative — leaving it standing because it was flattering — is how a project like this becomes worthless. Everything here is dated, and anything we retract stays visible with the reason.

The seven-minute problem — and the seven-hour opportunity

The hardest fact in this event is that the people closest to the mountain could not have been saved by any warning system that has to involve a human being. Seven minutes forty is not enough to notice, decide, call, and have someone run. For the border crossing, only a machine deciding on its own — a seismometer wired directly to a siren — would have worked.

But that is not true of the rest of the river. Betrawati had 43 minutes. Galchhi had over two hours. Devghat had seven hours. Those are ordinary warning timescales, comfortably enough for phone trees, sirens and evacuation, and the towns and hydropower sites along that stretch held far more people than the first 22 kilometres did. Where the dead actually were is not something we can say from a model, and the district-by-district breakdown was not published at the time of writing; the point here is about the time available, not about apportioning a toll.

The seismic network detected the collapse within minutes. It was logged as an earthquake, and the SMS warning went out 38 minutes later. It is worth being careful about what that does and does not mean. Automatically classifying a landslide seismic signature in real time and routing it to a downstream flood warning is something the research literature says is feasible — GFZ's Niels Hovius has put the achievable delay at minutes — but it is not, as far as we can find, running operationally in any national warning system, and the signal looks like a small earthquake because that is very nearly what it is. Nepal's forecasters were also losing their instruments while it happened: four gauges on this river were destroyed by the flood, two of them inside the first hour. So this is a gap in how these systems are designed generally rather than something that went wrong in one control room, and it is a much cheaper gap to close than new monitoring hardware — the sensors it would need are already in the ground.

An independent audit by geopera of whether this collapse could have been predicted came back null on all four satellite channels: under two metres of creep in a hundred days, steady, with no acceleration in the final 48 hours; ordinary glacier thinning; and a warm summer nearly identical to the two before it that produced no collapse. You cannot watch every peak. But you do not have to predict the collapse to reach the people two hours downstream — you only have to notice it and route it. The hours between the collapse and the flood's arrival at the lower river were real, and no part of the system was built to use them. That is a statement about how these systems are designed everywhere, not about anyone's conduct on the day.

What we're not sure of

The honesty box

Retracted or reversed this week, in order: the Seti River test, entirely; the idea that a briefly dammed river could have supplied the flood's volume (blocking that river for 37 hours would be needed, and it was minutes); reading the 70-metre mud lines as flow depth; then over-correcting and reading them as pure run-up; and a rule-of-thumb for wave volume that assumed the wave travelled as a rigid block, which our own earlier work had already disproved.

Corrected on 5 September, after reading these pages cold: the melt ceiling was stated as sitting "far below" the official 20 million cubic metres while the chart on this page showed 15 against 20 — carrying our own collapse-size envelope into the energy budget, which should have happened the day that envelope existed, puts it at 1.1–2.5 and makes the claim true; a bar on that chart labelled "river already lying in the channel, 47" was actually the whole wave at Devghat, and the standing channel water is 22.4; and the border-clock resolution was written with more confidence than its sources carry, which is now set out above rather than left for you to discover.

Corrected on 6 September, evening, after a second cold read: the cliff box above compared the mud-line speed of 54 with the old clock's 52 and called it a match "within a few per cent" — against the corrected 47.8 it is within about 15%, and the box now says so; the number of speed estimates at the border was counted wrongly; the opening of this page still gave a toll ten days old; "only something running like slush" claimed more than the test shows; and "wall of water" was the wrong description of what reached the border. We also stopped using the word "survivors" for the model runs that pass our tests. On a page about an event with 1,342 dead it should never have been used for anything else.

The size of the collapse is genuinely unknown. Published estimates span a factor of four hundred. Our envelope is much narrower, but it comes from requiring consistency across several measurements rather than from measuring the mountain, and it depends on a debris-volume figure that is itself a lower bound taken from less than half the valley floor.

Our model puts the debris in the wrong place. Satellite stereo shows the main deposit 40–43 km downstream, where the valley opens. Ours drops it too early. geopera's model got this right and ours did not.

One number in the two-solids fix is fitted, not derived — the split between coarse and fine. We say so rather than bury it, and we kept two downstream measurements out of the fitting so they could act as an honest check. They improved sharply, which is the evidence that the fix is physics rather than curve-fitting.

We ran one last test and it half failed. Two downstream measurements were deliberately kept out of the fitting so they could act as an honest check. The peak discharge at Devghat met its pass criterion — within a factor of two — for every passing version, but all 26 of them come out below the observation (1,752–2,889 m³/s against about 2,900), so what that test really says is "not contradicted", not "reproduced". The height the river rose at Galchhi came out at about 3.6 metres against roughly 9 observed. The model routes about the right amount of water and then converts it into the wrong height, which points at our estimate of the river's shape in its lower reaches rather than at the flood physics. Below the halfway point we have no measured cross-sections, only a rule of thumb. We are publishing that failure rather than quietly dropping the test.

The size estimate is probably too small. Late on 6 September, the volume that ran up the Chinese arm and a 45–70 m mud line at the Upper Trishuli-1 headworks both said so, by routes that share nothing with the estimate. The details are under "How big was the collapse". The estimate stands with that caveat until the model is re-run against water depths, which it has never been scored on.

The border speed is the number we fit worst, and it is one of the inputs the collapse-size envelope was scored against. Five estimates exist — 54, 45–52, our own video reading of 47, our routing fit of 31, and the peer-reviewed 19 — and we scored against the 45–52. That is a real open exposure, not a rounding error.

This is fast, independent, AI-assisted analysis published days after a disaster. It has not been peer-reviewed, and no Nepali scientist has read it. Where our numbers disagree with an agency's, both are shown. Where others got somewhere first or got it better, we say so — geopera's satellite work is better than ours, and their sediment result arrived independently and agrees with ours, though both analyses are unreviewed.

How this was made

The method, plainly

Dave Hume, an engineer in New Zealand, working in the evenings over ten days with Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic) running in Claude Code, a terminal tool where the model can write and run programs, read files, search the web and publish pages. The AI wrote the physics code, ran the simulations, processed the satellite imagery and drafted the prose; the human set the direction, brought the original hypothesis and did most of the catching. It is on the page rather than in a footnote so that you can discount it if you think that changes what the work is worth.

What it was bad at, since that is the part worth knowing: confident errors in its own fresh work. Sediment counted twice in the ledger; a chart whose bars were drawn to the wrong axis; a bar on this page's own melt chart mislabelled for three days. All were in things written minutes earlier and believed, and none would have been caught by asking the model whether it was sure. Every substantive correction on this site was found by a human pushing back or by re-reading the pages cold, never by the model auditing itself.

Rules we worked under, kept in the repository since day one: state conclusions as ranges over disputed inputs rather than single numbers; never tune a parameter to fit the very observation you are scoring against, and if you must, declare it and hold other measurements back as a check; report failures with their diagnostic shape rather than hiding them; credit the researchers who got there first — the contribution here is arithmetic, not correction. Where those rules were broken, the breach is on the page. A longer account of the division of labour, including the specific errors and who caught them, is in PLAN.md rather than here.

Checkable. Every number on this page comes from code in a public repository that runs on open data — seconds for the budgets, minutes for the routing, about half an hour for the size envelope. The executed workings are here, and the full development history, including every retraction and the reasoning behind it, is in the commit log. Nothing rests on trusting either of us.

What would help most

Three things, and none of them require new instruments:

The full gauge records from Galchhi and Devghat, held by Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM). A photograph has no time axis, so no amount of satellite imagery can measure how much water passed. Only a gauge trace can, and it would settle the volume argument everyone is having.

Hydropower plant records — headworks sensors, gate logs, control-system archives across the thirteen affected projects, including plants that were damaged but whose data survived off-site. These plausibly hold the only high-frequency measurements of the flood's upper reach, where the public gauges were destroyed.

Repeat satellite elevation surveys over the next few weeks. If the deposit was ice-rich, it must go on losing volume as buried ice melts out. Rock doesn't do that. Two surveys a month apart would separate the two directly, with no model involved.

The one-paragraph version

In short A mountainside of rock and ice fell into a small gorge and, seven minutes forty later, arrived at a monsoon-swollen river at 170 km/h — the mountain itself, not yet a flood. It could not have melted enough ice to make the flood — at any collapse size the evidence supports, the energy of the fall falls short by a factor of eight — so the water was the river's own, swept up and delivered at once. The collapse reached a border camera 22 km away in seven minutes forty, which dry rock cannot do at any size; ice is visible in the footage, and the run is what an ice-rich avalanche does on its own, lubricated the way a skate is; how much of the mass was ice is still open. Mud lines on a cliff where the flow hit head-on give the wave's depth and speed by a route that never touches the clock, and they agree with it. Our estimate of the collapse is an order of magnitude below the largest published figures, and two measurements made late on 6 September say it is probably too small; it is under revision. Our model, given the ability to scour a valley, matched the measured erosion — and then failed a test we designed to break it, which told us the flood was carrying two different kinds of sediment, not one. For the first 22 km no human warning chain could have worked. For the next 177 there were hours, and no system yet exists anywhere that would have turned a seismic signal into a downstream warning inside them.
